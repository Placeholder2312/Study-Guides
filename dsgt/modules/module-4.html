1 · Pigeonhole Principle (4.1)

Teacher’s framing: 20 pigeons, 19 holes

Suppose 20 pigeons fly into 19 pigeonholes. Because there are more pigeons than holes, at least one hole must contain at least two pigeons.

Proof: if every hole held at most one pigeon, at most 19 pigeons could be accommodated. We have 20. Therefore at least one hole holds two or more.

That is the entire principle. It is a proof technique: you use it to show that something must exist, without constructing it. You prove existence by contradiction.

Basic vs Generalized — the difference

Basic Pigeonhole Principle: If k + 1 or more objects are placed into k boxes, at least one box contains two or more objects.

Generalized Pigeonhole Principle: If n objects are placed into m boxes, at least one box contains at least ⌈n/m⌉ objects.

⌈ ⌉ is the ceiling function: round up. 21 objects into 10 boxes → ⌈21/10⌉ = 3.

The basic form only tells you that one box has ≥ 2. The generalized form tells you the minimum count in the fullest box. If the question asks “at least how many,” use the generalized form.

Guarantee Questions — Formula

To guarantee that at least k objects are in one box, when there are m boxes:

n = m(k − 1) + 1

m = number of boxes / categories  ·  k = number of objects that must be guaranteed in at least one box  ·  n = minimum number of objects required.

Why: with m(k − 1) objects you can put exactly k − 1 in each box and avoid the guarantee. The +1 forces it.

2 · Inclusion-Exclusion Principle (4.2)

Source: LibreTexts, “Other Basic Counting Techniques”

When groups overlap, adding their sizes counts the shared elements twice. Inclusion-exclusion corrects this: add the sizes of the individual groups, then subtract the sizes of their overlaps.

If two sets A and B do not overlap, |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B|. If they do overlap, |A| + |B| counts |A ∩ B| twice, once in A and once in B. Subtracting the overlap once gives the correct total.

Source: LibreTexts, three-set union

For three sets A, B, C, the pairwise intersections A ∩ B, A ∩ C, B ∩ C are overcounted. Subtracting all three overcompensates, because A ∩ B ∩ C has been subtracted too many times, so it must be added back in. The signs alternate: add singles, subtract pairs, add triples.

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|

Two sets: add both, subtract the overlap.

|A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|

Three sets: add singles, subtract pairs, add triples.

Three-Set Venn Diagram — All Seven Regions

Pair labels (A∩B etc.) mean those two sets only, not the third. The seven regions: Only A, Only B, Only C, A & B only, A & C only, B & C only, A & B & C.

3 · Recurrence Relations (4.3)

Source: Oscar Levin, “Discrete Mathematics: An Open Introduction,” Section 2.4

A recurrence relation expresses each term of a sequence in terms of previous terms. Your teacher’s form: T(n) = T(n−1) + f(n). A recurrence relation is a recursive definition without initial conditions. With initial conditions, it defines the entire sequence.

Important: a recurrence relation requires a sufficient set of initial conditions to uniquely determine the sequence. For a second-order recurrence (one involving aₙ₋₁ and aₙ₋₂), two initial values are normally required — typically a₀ and a₁.

Recursive definitions are often easier to find than closed formulas. Solving a recurrence means finding a function of n, a closed formula, that satisfies both the recurrence and the initial conditions. This is analogous to solving differential equations: finding the solution may be difficult, but verifying it is easy: just substitute it back in.

Source: Oscar Levin, Characteristic Root Technique (Section 2.4)

Consider aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 6aₙ₋₂, or aₙ − aₙ₋₁ − 6aₙ₋₂ = 0. Iteration becomes unwieldy, so guess a geometric form rⁿ. Substituting gives rⁿ − rⁿ⁻¹ − 6rⁿ⁻² = 0. If r ≠ 0, divide by the smallest power of r to get r² − r − 6 = 0, which factors to (r − 3)(r + 2) = 0. Thus r = 3 or r = −2. Both (−2)ⁿ and 3ⁿ are solutions, as is any linear combination a(−2)ⁿ + b·3ⁿ. The polynomial r² − r − 6 is the characteristic equation; its roots are the characteristic roots. If r = 0, check it separately as a possible root.

How to Form and Solve the Characteristic Equation

  1. Write the recurrence in standard form: aₙ − c₁aₙ₋₁ − c₂aₙ₋₂ = 0.
  2. Assume a solution aₙ = rⁿ and substitute.
  3. If r ≠ 0, divide by the smallest power of r to get the characteristic equation (a polynomial in r). Check r = 0 separately as a possible root.
  4. Factorise the characteristic equation and find its roots r₁, r₂.
  5. Construct the general solution based on the roots (distinct or repeated).
  6. Use the initial conditions to solve for the constants.
  7. Verify by substituting back into the recurrence.

Case A: Two Distinct Real Roots

If the characteristic equation has distinct roots r₁ and r₂, the solution is:

aₙ = A(r₁ⁿ) + B(r₂ⁿ)

A and B are determined by initial conditions.

Teacher’s recurrence 1 — repeated roots

aₙ = 6aₙ₋₁ − 9aₙ₋₂, a₀ = 1, a₁ = 4

Standard form: aₙ − 6aₙ₋₁ + 9aₙ₋₂ = 0

Characteristic: r² − 6r + 9 = 0 → (r − 3)² = 0 → root 3, repeated

General: aₙ = A(3ⁿ) + Bn(3ⁿ)

  • n=0: A = 1
  • n=1: 3A + 3B = 4 → 3 + 3B = 4 → B = 1/3
aₙ = 3ⁿ + (n/3)·3ⁿ

Verify: a₂ = 6(4) − 9(1) = 24 − 9 = 15. Formula: 9 + (2/3)·9 = 9 + 6 = 15. ✓

Teacher’s recurrence 2 — distinct roots

aₙ = 5aₙ₋₁ + 14aₙ₋₂, a₀ = 2, a₁ = 5

Standard form: aₙ − 5aₙ₋₁ − 14aₙ₋₂ = 0

Characteristic: r² − 5r − 14 = 0 → (r − 7)(r + 2) = 0 → roots 7, −2

General: aₙ = A(7ⁿ) + B(−2)ⁿ

  • n=0: A + B = 2
  • n=1: 7A − 2B = 5

From A = 2 − B: 7(2 − B) − 2B = 5 → 14 − 7B − 2B = 5 → −9B = −9 → B = 1, A = 1.

aₙ = 7ⁿ + (−2)ⁿ

Verify: a₂ = 5(5) + 14(2) = 25 + 28 = 53. Formula: 49 + 4 = 53. ✓

Teacher’s recurrence 3 — distinct roots

aₙ = 5aₙ₋₁ + 6aₙ₋₂, a₀ = 3, a₁ = 13

Standard form: aₙ − 5aₙ₋₁ − 6aₙ₋₂ = 0

Characteristic: r² − 5r − 6 = 0 → (r − 6)(r + 1) = 0 → roots 6, −1

General: aₙ = A(6ⁿ) + B(−1)ⁿ

  • n=0: A + B = 3
  • n=1: 6A − B = 13

Adding: 7A = 16 → A = 16/7. Then B = 3 − 16/7 = 5/7.

aₙ = (16/7)·6ⁿ + (5/7)·(−1)ⁿ

Verify: a₂ = 5(13) + 6(3) = 65 + 18 = 83. Formula: (16/7)·36 + (5/7)·1 = 576/7 + 5/7 = 581/7 = 83. ✓

Case B: Repeated Root

If the characteristic polynomial has only one root r (it factors as (x − r)²), then rⁿ is a solution, but aₙ = ar₁ⁿ + br₂ⁿ cannot satisfy all initial conditions because r₁ⁿ and r₂ⁿ are indistinguishable. The solution is:

aₙ = arⁿ + bnrⁿ

The extra n in bnrⁿ allows solving for both constants.

Case C: Non-Homogeneous (Extra Term on the Right)

Exam-relevant: Summer 2026 Q5(b) is non-homogeneous (solved in the Mumbai University section below). The particular-solution guessing method is added in this guide and is not from Levin’s Section 2.4.

When an extra term appears on the right: aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂ + f(n):

  1. Solve the homogeneous part (ignore f(n)) → aₙ⁽ʰ⁾.
  2. Guess a particular solution aₙ⁽ᵖ⁾ based on the form of f(n).
  3. General solution: aₙ = aₙ⁽ʰ⁾ + aₙ⁽ᵖ⁾.
  4. Apply initial conditions.

Constant kC
Linear an + bCn + D
kⁿ (k is not a characteristic root)C · kⁿ
kⁿ (k is a characteristic root)C · n · kⁿ (C · n² · kⁿ if k is a double root)

If your guess matches the homogeneous solution, multiply by n until it does not. Example: for f(n) = 7·5ⁿ with roots 5 and −2, 5 is a root, so guess C·n·5ⁿ.

Additional practice — non-homogeneous with constant right side

aₙ₊₂ − 5aₙ₊₁ + 6aₙ = 2, a₀ = 1, a₁ = −1

Homogeneous: r² − 5r + 6 = 0 → roots 2, 3 → aₙ⁽ʰ⁾ = A(2ⁿ) + B(3ⁿ)

Particular guess (constant): K − 5K + 6K = 2 → 2K = 2 → K = 1

General: aₙ = A(2ⁿ) + B(3ⁿ) + 1

  • n=0: A + B + 1 = 1 → A + B = 0
  • n=1: 2A + 3B + 1 = −1 → 2A + 3B = −2

From A = −B: −2B + 3B = −2 → B = −2, A = 2.

aₙ = 2·2ⁿ − 2·3ⁿ + 1

Verify: a₂ = 5(−1) − 6(1) + 2 = −9. Formula: 8 − 18 + 1 = −9. ✓

Additional practice — distinct roots with negative answer

aₙ − 9aₙ₋₁ + 20aₙ₋₂ = 0, a₀ = −3, a₁ = −10

Characteristic: r² − 9r + 20 = 0 → (r − 4)(r − 5) = 0 → roots 4, 5

General: aₙ = A(4ⁿ) + B(5ⁿ)

  • n=0: A + B = −3
  • n=1: 4A + 5B = −10

From A = −3 − B: 4(−3 − B) + 5B = −10 → −12 − 4B + 5B = −10 → B = 2, A = −5.

aₙ = −5·4ⁿ + 2·5ⁿ
Additional practice — third-order recurrence (not a confirmed teacher-provided exam pattern)

cₙ = 6cₙ₋₁ − 11cₙ₋₂ + 6cₙ₋₃, c₀ = 2, c₁ = 5, c₂ = 15

Characteristic: r³ − 6r² + 11r − 6 = 0. Try r = 1: 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0, so r = 1 is a root. Factor out (r − 1): (r − 1)(r² − 5r + 6) = 0 → roots 1, 2, 3

General: cₙ = A + B(2ⁿ) + C(3ⁿ)

  • n=0: A + B + C = 2
  • n=1: A + 2B + 3C = 5
  • n=2: A + 4B + 9C = 15

Subtracting row 1 from row 2: B + 2C = 3. Subtracting row 2 from row 3: 2B + 6C = 10 → B + 3C = 5. Subtracting: C = 2, B = −1, A = 1.

cₙ = 1 − 2ⁿ + 2·3ⁿ

Winter 2025 — Q1(c): Programming Contest — Pigeonhole Principle (5 marks)
Source: NEP DSGT Winter 2025 — Q1(c)

A programming contest has 240 participants and 12 judging terminals.

  1. Using Pigeonhole Principle, find the minimum number of contestants who must end up using the same terminal.
  2. Explain how this insight helps in contest scheduling, load balancing, and avoiding terminal bottlenecks.

Solution:

Pigeons (n) = 240 participants  ·  Holes (m) = 12 judging terminals

⌈240 / 12⌉ = ⌈20⌉ = 20

At least 20 participants must end up using the same terminal.

Reasoning: If we try to distribute 240 participants evenly across 12 terminals, each terminal gets exactly 20 participants. If every terminal had at most 19 participants, the total would be at most 19 × 12 = 228, which is less than 240. So at least one terminal has 20 or more participants.

Application: This insight helps in contest scheduling by ensuring that at least one terminal will have a full load of 20 participants, allowing organizers to plan for worst-case load balancing and avoid terminal bottlenecks by provisioning adequate resources at the busiest terminal.

Winter 2025 — Q2(a): Campus Event App — Inclusion-Exclusion (8 marks)
Source: NEP DSGT Winter 2025 — Q2(a)

A campus event app records students who use three app sections:

CategoryCount
E (Event Announcements)140
C (Clubs & Communities)110
R (Reels / Short videos)120
E ∩ C65
E ∩ R70
C ∩ R55
Total Students200
  1. Using inclusion-exclusion, compute how many students use all three sections.
  2. Find students who use only one section.
  3. Explain how this analysis helps app developers target notifications and improve UI.

Solution:

Let |E ∪ C ∪ R| = 200 (every student uses at least one section).

Using inclusion-exclusion:

|E ∪ C ∪ R| = |E| + |C| + |R| − |E ∩ C| − |E ∩ R| − |C ∩ R| + |E ∩ C ∩ R|
200 = 140 + 110 + 120 − 65 − 70 − 55 + |E ∩ C ∩ R|
200 = 370 − 190 + |E ∩ C ∩ R|
200 = 180 + |E ∩ C ∩ R| → |E ∩ C ∩ R| = 20

20 students use all three sections.

Only one section:

  • Only E = 140 − 65 − 70 + 20 = 25
  • Only C = 110 − 65 − 55 + 20 = 10
  • Only R = 120 − 70 − 55 + 20 = 15
  • Total only one = 25 + 10 + 15 = 50 students

Application: App developers can target notifications to the 25 students who only use Event Announcements, 10 who only use Clubs & Communities, and 15 who only use Reels. UI improvements can prioritize the most-used section per user segment.

Winter 2025 — Q5(b): Student Club Followers — Recurrence Relation (8 marks)
Source: NEP DSGT Winter 2025 — Q5(b)

A student club records followers using recurrence: Fₙ = 4Fₙ₋₁ − 4Fₙ₋₂ with F₀ = 10 (core team) and F₁ = 30 (after first promotion).

  1. Solve the recurrence relation.
  2. Describe the growth pattern (linear/exponential).
  3. Comment whether such growth is realistic for a campus club in long term.

Solution:

Standard form: aₙ − 4aₙ₋₁ + 4aₙ₋₂ = 0

Characteristic: r² − 4r + 4 = 0 → (r − 2)² = 0 → root 2, repeated

General: aₙ = A(2ⁿ) + Bn(2ⁿ)

  • n=0: A = 10
  • n=1: 2A + 2B = 30 → 20 + 2B = 30 → B = 5
Fₙ = 10·2ⁿ + 5n·2ⁿ = (10 + 5n)·2ⁿ

Growth pattern: Exponential growth with a linear factor (10 + 5n). The dominant term is 5n·2ⁿ, which grows exponentially.

Realistic? No. Exponential growth of followers is not realistic for a campus club in the long term because the total student population is finite. Eventually, the club will saturate the available student population and growth will slow down (logistic growth rather than exponential).

Summer 2026 — Q1(c): Socks Drawer — Pigeonhole Principle (5 marks)
Source: NEP DSGT Summer 2026 — Q1(c)

A quality control department stores 10 pairs of socks of different colors in a drawer for packaging verification.

  1. Using the Pigeonhole Principle, find the minimum number of socks that must be selected randomly to guarantee that at least one matching pair of the same color is obtained.
  2. Justify your answer with suitable reasoning.

Solution:

Pigeons = socks pulled out  ·  Holes = 10 colors

To guarantee at least 2 socks of the same color (k = 2):

n = m(k − 1) + 1 = 10(2 − 1) + 1 = 11

11 socks must be pulled out to guarantee a matching pair.

Reasoning: With 10 socks, you could pull exactly one sock of each color (10 different colors, no match). The 11th sock must match one of the previously pulled socks because there are only 10 colors available.

Summer 2026 — Q5(b): Financial Portfolio — Non-Homogeneous Recurrence (7 marks)
Source: NEP DSGT Summer 2026 — Q5(b)

A financial analytics company models the quarterly growth of a digital investment portfolio using: Fₙ = 3Fₙ₋₁ + 10Fₙ₋₂ + 7·5ⁿ with F₀ = 4, F₁ = −3.

  1. Solve the recurrence relation.
  2. Determine the general expression for Fₙ.
  3. Analyze the effect of the exponential forcing term 7·5ⁿ.

Solution:

Homogeneous part: r² − 3r − 10 = 0 → (r − 5)(r + 2) = 0 → roots 5, −2

Homogeneous solution: aₙ⁽ʰ⁾ = A(5ⁿ) + B(−2)ⁿ

Particular solution: forcing term is 7·5ⁿ. Since 5 is a root of the characteristic equation, guess aₙ⁽ᵖ⁾ = Cn·5ⁿ:

Cn·5ⁿ = 3·C(n−1)·5ⁿ⁻¹ + 10·C(n−2)·5ⁿ⁻² + 7·5ⁿ
Divide by 5ⁿ⁻²: 25Cn = 15C(n−1) + 10C(n−2) + 175
25Cn = 15Cn − 15C + 10Cn − 20C + 175
25Cn = 25Cn − 35C + 175
35C = 175 → C = 5

General solution: Fₙ = A(5ⁿ) + B(−2)ⁿ + 5n·5ⁿ

  • n=0: A + B = 4
  • n=1: 5A − 2B + 25 = −3 → 5A − 2B = −28

From A = 4 − B: 5(4 − B) − 2B = −28 → 20 − 5B − 2B = −28 → −7B = −48 → B = 48/7

A = 4 − 48/7 = (28 − 48)/7 = −20/7

Fₙ = (−20/7)·5ⁿ + (48/7)·(−2)ⁿ + 5n·5ⁿ

Analysis: The forcing term 7·5ⁿ introduces an exponential growth component 5n·5ⁿ which dominates as n increases. Since 5 is both a characteristic root and the base of the forcing term, the particular solution includes the extra factor n.

Syllabus Self-Learning Topics

These are syllabus self-learning topics. They are not presented as confirmed exam questions.

Applications of recurrence relations in analysing recursive algorithms

Recurrence relations model the running time of recursive algorithms. For example, a divide-and-conquer algorithm that splits a problem of size n into two subproblems of size n/2 and does O(n) work to combine them satisfies T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n, which solves to O(n log n).

Applications of counting techniques in probability

Counting techniques (permutations, combinations, inclusion-exclusion) are used to compute probabilities when all outcomes are equally likely: P(event) = (favourable outcomes) / (total outcomes). For example, the probability of drawing a specific hand of cards uses combinations.

Applications of counting techniques in decision-making

Counting techniques help in decision-making under uncertainty, such as enumerating possible schedules, resource allocations, or outcomes in games to choose the option with the best expected result.

  1. In a class of 40 students, 25 play cricket, 20 play football, and 10 play both. How many play at least one sport? How many play neither?
  2. 100 students take Mathematics, Physics, or Chemistry. 50 take Maths, 40 take Physics, 30 take Chemistry. 15 take M&P, 10 take M&C, 8 take P&C, and 5 take all three. How many take at least one subject?
  3. How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by neither 2 nor 3?
  4. Three sets A, B, C: |A|=40, |B|=35, |C|=30, |A∩B|=12, |A∩C|=10, |B∩C|=8, |A∩B∩C|=5. How many elements are in exactly two of the sets?
  5. In a school of 200 students, 80 are in Band, 60 in Choir, 50 in Orchestra. 30 are in B&C, 25 in B&O, 20 in C&O, and 10 in all three. How many are in none?
  6. 200 students use a campus event app: 140 use Events, 110 use Clubs, 120 use Reels. E∩C=65, E∩R=70, C∩R=55. How many use all three?
  7. 1000 patients in a drug trial: 122 symptom A, 88 symptom B, 112 symptom C. A∩B=27, A∩C=29, B∩C=32, A∩B∩C=10. How many have at least one symptom?

Pigeonhole Principle

  1. 2 people. 13 people into 12 months → ⌈13/12⌉ = 2.
  2. 8 students. n = 7(2 − 1) + 1 = 8.
  3. 3 socks. Worst case: 1 black + 1 blue. The 3rd sock must match one.
  4. Two integers share a remainder mod 5. 6 integers into 5 remainders → two share remainder r, so 5 | (a − b).
  5. 57 people. n = 7(9 − 1) + 1 = 57.
  6. 20 participants. ⌈240 / 12⌉ = 20.
  7. 11 socks. n = 10(2 − 1) + 1 = 11.

Inclusion-Exclusion Principle

  1. 35 play at least one; 5 play neither. |C ∪ F| = 25 + 20 − 10 = 35. Neither = 40 − 35 = 5.
  2. 92. (50+40+30) − (15+10+8) + 5 = 120 − 33 + 5 = 92.
  3. 33. Divisible by 2: 50; by 3: 33; by 6: 16. Union = 50 + 33 − 16 = 67. Neither = 100 − 67 = 33.
  4. 15. Exactly two = |A∩B| + |A∩C| + |B∩C| − 3|A∩B∩C| = 12 + 10 + 8 − 15 = 15.
  5. 75. Union = (80+60+50) − (30+25+20) + 10 = 125. None = 200 − 125 = 75.
  6. 20 students use all three sections. |E ∪ C ∪ R| = 140 + 110 + 120 − 65 − 70 − 55 + |E ∩ C ∩ R| = 200 → |E ∩ C ∩ R| = 20.
  7. 244 patients develop at least one symptom. |A ∪ B ∪ C| = 122 + 88 + 112 − 27 − 29 − 32 + 10 = 244.

Common Mistakes

Pigeonhole

  • Confusing basic PHP (≥ 2 exists) with generalized PHP (minimum count in fullest box).
  • Using the wrong formula for guarantee questions — use n = m(k − 1) + 1, not just ⌈n/m⌉.

Inclusion-Exclusion

  • Forgetting to add back |A ∩ B ∩ C| on three-set problems.
  • Confusing “exactly two” with “at least two.”

Recurrence

  • Leaving out the particular solution when the right side is non-zero.
  • Forgetting that a second-order recurrence needs two initial conditions.
  • Not verifying the closed formula by substituting back into the recurrence.

Patterns from the Question Papers

  • Pigeonhole, 5 marks: ⌈n / m⌉ (Winter 2025 Q1(c)) or m(k − 1) + 1 (Summer 2026 Q1(c)), plus a short justification.
  • Inclusion-exclusion, 8 marks: a three-set table (Winter 2025 Q2(a), Summer 2026 Q2(a)). Find the union or the triple intersection, then the “only one” counts, then a Venn diagram.
  • Recurrence, 7–8 marks: repeated root (Winter 2025 Q5(b)) or non-homogeneous with the extra factor n (Summer 2026 Q5(b)).
  • Most parts end with an “explain how this helps” sub-question. Write one or two sentences on the real-world use.
