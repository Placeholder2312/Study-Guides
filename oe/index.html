  2. Use 3–5 meaningful headings: causes / process / effects / measures, as relevant.

India faces multiple forms of malnutrition: undernutrition (stunting, wasting and underweight), micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight/obesity. “Hidden hunger” means a person may consume enough calories but still lack essential vitamins or minerals such as iron, iodine, vitamin A or zinc. What is the Montreal Protocol? State its main objective.

Show recall cue

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

The Montreal Protocol is an international environmental agreement adopted in 1987 to protect the ozone layer. Explain the impact of Environmental pollution on Human Health. (8 marks)

Show recall cue

Types → exposure routes → health effects → vulnerable groups → prevention.

Answer skeleton
  1. Pollution enters the body through air, water, food and other exposures; harm depends on the pollutant, dose, duration and route.
  2. Air pollution, especially PM₂.₅, is linked to respiratory and cardiovascular disease, stroke and lung cancer, with children and older people most vulnerable.
  3. Water, soil/food and noise pollution cause water-borne disease, chemical organ damage, and hearing, sleep and concentration problems.
  4. Control at source: emission and effluent standards, sewage and waste treatment, clean transport and energy, monitoring and safe drinking water.

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

Environmental pollution harms health when people breathe contaminated air, drink polluted water, eat contaminated food or are exposed to toxic substances and excessive noise. Its effects depend on the pollutant, dose, duration and route of exposure.

1. Air pollution: Fine particles such as PM₂.₅ can travel deep into the lungs and may enter the bloodstream. Exposure is associated with asthma and other respiratory infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart disease, stroke and lung cancer. Children, older people and those with existing illnesses are especially vulnerable. [S16]

2. Water pollution: Sewage and disease-causing microorganisms can spread diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid and other water-borne diseases. Chemical contaminants such as arsenic, lead and mercury can damage organs and affect the nervous system.

3. Soil and food contamination: Pesticides, industrial chemicals and heavy metals can enter crops and the food chain. Long-term exposure may affect the nervous system, kidneys, development and reproductive health, depending on the substance.

4. Noise and other pollution: Excessive noise can cause hearing problems, sleep disturbance, stress and reduced concentration.

Control measures: Enforce emission and effluent standards; treat sewage and industrial wastewater; improve public transport and clean energy; manage solid and hazardous waste scientifically; monitor air and water quality; provide safe drinking water; and educate communities.

Final answer

Pollution is not only an environmental problem; it is a public-health issue. Prevention at the source, monitoring and strong enforcement protect both people and ecosystems.

Apply the concept of the carbon footprint to explain how industries and transportation contribute to global warming. Suggest methods for reducing their carbon footprint. (7 marks)

Show recall cue

Answer skeleton
  1. A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse-gas emissions of an activity or organisation in CO₂e, including direct and indirect emissions.
  2. Industry burns coal, oil and gas and releases process CO₂ (for example cement), with fuel extraction and supply chains adding emissions.
  3. Transport burns petrol, diesel and aviation fuel; congestion and inefficient vehicles increase fuel use.
  4. Extra greenhouse gases strengthen the greenhouse effect and cause global warming.
  5. Reduce through efficiency, renewables, electrification, lower-carbon materials, public transport, cycling, rail, electric vehicles and better logistics.

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse-gas emissions associated with an activity or organisation, expressed as carbon-dioxide equivalent (CO₂e). It includes direct emissions and, depending on the boundary used, indirect emissions from purchased energy and supply chains. [S6]

Industries: Factories and power plants burn coal, oil and gas to produce heat and electricity, releasing carbon dioxide. Some industrial processes, such as cement production, also release CO₂ through chemical reactions. Extraction and transport of fuels, leaks and energy-intensive supply chains add further emissions.

Transportation: Cars, buses, trucks, ships and aircraft burn petrol, diesel, aviation fuel or other fossil fuels. Combustion releases CO₂; congestion and inefficient vehicles increase fuel use. Freight and personal travel therefore add to the carbon footprint.

How this causes global warming: Greenhouse gases absorb some outgoing infrared radiation from Earth and re-emit energy. Increasing their concentration strengthens the greenhouse effect and changes the climate system. Human activities, especially greenhouse-gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming. [S7, S8]

Reduction methods: Industries can improve energy efficiency, recover waste heat, electrify processes where possible, use renewable electricity, adopt lower-carbon materials, reduce process emissions, repair gas leaks and measure supply-chain emissions. Transport emissions can be reduced by reliable public transport, walking and cycling infrastructure, rail and efficient freight, shared mobility, electric vehicles powered by cleaner electricity, fuel-efficient vehicles and better logistics.

Final answer

Measuring the footprint helps identify the largest sources; reducing fossil-fuel use and improving efficiency in industry and transport are central climate solutions.

India’s environmental priorities include improving air quality, securing safe drinking water, protecting rivers and groundwater, restoring forests and biodiversity, preventing land degradation, managing waste and adapting to climate risks. The Ganges is a major river system supporting drinking water, irrigation, fisheries, transport, cultural practices and livelihoods. Its pollution is caused by several sources operating across towns, cities and industrial areas along the river and its tributaries.

Section B · Descriptive · 7 marks

Describe the causes of acidification and explain suitable mitigation measures for affected lakes, soils and forests. (7 marks)

Show recall cue

SO₂ + NOₓ → sulfuric/nitric acids → deposition → ecosystem damage → reduce emissions and restore.

Answer skeleton
  1. Acidification occurs when acidic deposition exceeds natural buffering; SO₂ and NOₓ from power plants, industry, vehicles and fuel combustion form sulphuric and nitric acids.
  2. Acids return by rain, snow, fog or dry deposition; lakes lose sensitive fish, eggs, plankton and insects, and aluminium becomes toxic.
  3. Soils leach calcium and magnesium and mobilise aluminium; trees suffer root and leaf damage and become less resilient.
  4. Mitigation: reduce SO₂ and NOₓ at source through low-sulphur fuel, scrubbers, low-NOₓ combustion, catalytic reduction, cleaner energy, efficiency and stricter standards.
  5. Restoration: scientifically assessed liming, forest restoration, soil testing, catchment protection and long-term monitoring, not a substitute for emission reduction.

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

Acidification occurs when lakes, soils and forests receive acidic substances faster than their natural buffering capacity can neutralise them. A major cause is the release of sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) from coal-fired power plants, industries, vehicles and fuel combustion. In the atmosphere these gases react with water, oxygen and other chemicals to form sulphuric and nitric acids. The acids return to Earth through rain, snow, fog or dry deposition. [S24]

Effects on lakes: Lower pH harms sensitive fish, eggs, plankton and aquatic insects. Acidic water can also release aluminium from surrounding soils, which is toxic to aquatic organisms.

Effects on soils and forests: Acid deposition can leach essential nutrients such as calcium and magnesium from soil and mobilise aluminium. Tree roots may be damaged, leaves and needles may be injured, and trees become less resilient to drought, cold, pests and disease.

Mitigation: The most important solution is to reduce SO₂ and NOₓ at the source. Measures include low-sulphur fuels, flue-gas desulphurisation scrubbers, low-NOₓ combustion systems, catalytic reduction, cleaner energy, energy efficiency and stricter industrial and vehicle standards.

Restoration: Where appropriate and scientifically assessed, lakes and soils may be treated with lime or other alkaline materials to neutralise acidity. Forest restoration, soil testing, protection of catchments and long-term pH and biodiversity monitoring can help recovery. Liming is a local restoration measure, not a substitute for emission reduction. [S24]

Final answer

Acidification is best controlled by preventing acid-forming emissions and supporting recovery of affected ecosystems.

Explain how do deforestation, infrastructure development, and agriculture affect hotspots? (8 marks)

Show recall cue

Hotspot = unique species + threat; these activities remove, split and degrade habitat.

Answer skeleton
  1. Hotspots contain many endemic species and have lost at least 70% of original vegetation, so further disturbance is serious.
  2. Deforestation removes habitat, isolates patches, reduces breeding and gene flow, increases erosion and can increase human–wildlife conflict.
  3. Infrastructure fragments habitat: roads create barriers and mortality, dams alter flow and fish movement, and construction adds noise, light, pollution and human activity.
  4. Agriculture replaces forests and grasslands; pesticides and fertilisers pollute, monoculture reduces habitat diversity, and irrigation affects wetlands and rivers.
  5. Conserve core habitats and corridors, require rigorous EIA, avoid sensitive areas, use wildlife crossings and pollution controls, promote agroforestry and native restoration, and involve local communities.

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

Biodiversity hotspots contain many endemic species and have already lost at least 70% of their original natural vegetation. They are therefore highly sensitive to further habitat disturbance. [S10]

Deforestation: Clearing forests for timber, fuel, settlements or commercial use removes the habitat on which plants and animals depend. Small surviving forest patches become isolated, reducing breeding opportunities and gene flow. Loss of tree cover also increases erosion, changes local moisture and temperature and may increase human–wildlife conflict.

Infrastructure development: Roads, railways, dams, mines, power lines and urban expansion can divide continuous habitats into smaller fragments. Roads create barriers and wildlife mortality; dams alter river flow and fish movement; construction brings noise, light, pollution and human activity into previously less-disturbed areas. Poorly planned projects can disrupt wildlife corridors and breeding sites.

Agriculture: Expansion of cropland and plantations can replace natural forests and grasslands. Intensive farming may use pesticides and fertilisers that pollute soil and water. Monoculture reduces habitat diversity, while excessive irrigation and water extraction affect nearby wetlands and rivers.

Conservation measures: Protect core habitats and ecological corridors; require rigorous environmental impact assessment; avoid sensitive areas when alternatives exist; use wildlife crossings and pollution controls; promote agroforestry and sustainable farming; restore degraded areas with native species; and involve local and Indigenous communities in decisions. IPBES identifies land/sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution and invasive species as the five major direct drivers of biodiversity loss. [S15]

Final answer

Development and food production must be planned so that they do not destroy the ecological foundations of biodiversity hotspots.

Food security means that people have reliable access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food. In India, climate variability, soil degradation and water stress interact and can weaken food security.

Food security depends on healthy soil, reliable water and resilient ecosystems as well as agricultural production.

Section C · Descriptive · 8 marks

Explain how the enhanced greenhouse effect occurs and discuss any three impacts of rising atmospheric CO₂. (8 marks)

Show recall cue

Sun in → surface warms → infrared out → GHG absorption; more CO₂ = energy imbalance. Give 3 impacts.

Answer skeleton
  1. Natural greenhouse effect: sunlight warms the surface, the surface emits infrared radiation, and greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit energy, keeping Earth warm enough for life.
  2. Enhanced effect: burning coal, oil and gas, cement production and deforestation add greenhouse gases, which absorb more outgoing infrared energy and disturb Earth’s energy balance.
  3. Impact 1: higher average temperatures and more severe heat extremes affect health, work and crops.
  4. Impact 2: changing rainfall brings heavier rain and flood risk in some regions and longer or more intense drought and water stress in others.
  5. Impact 3: sea-level rise from ocean expansion and melting land ice increases coastal flooding and erosion; CO₂ also contributes to ocean acidification.

Self-test: say or write your answer from the cue, then reveal it and compare.

The natural greenhouse effect occurs when sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms Earth’s surface. The surface then emits energy as infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide absorb some of this outgoing radiation and re-emit energy, including back towards the surface. This natural process keeps Earth warm enough for life.

The enhanced greenhouse effect occurs when human activities—especially burning coal, oil and gas, cement production and deforestation—add extra greenhouse gases to the atmosphere. Higher concentrations absorb more outgoing infrared energy and disturb Earth’s energy balance, causing long-term warming. NASA explains that the natural greenhouse effect is essential, but the additional greenhouse gases from human activity trap extra heat. The IPCC identifies human greenhouse-gas emissions as the cause of observed global warming. [S7, S8]

Three impacts of rising atmospheric CO₂ and the resulting climate change:

1. Higher temperatures and heat extremes: Average temperatures rise and dangerous heat events become more severe, affecting health, work and crops.

2. Changing rainfall and water availability: Some regions experience heavier rainfall and flood risk, while others face longer or more intense drought and water stress.

3. Sea-level rise: Warmer ocean water expands and melting land ice adds water to the oceans, increasing coastal flooding and erosion risks.

CO₂ also dissolves into seawater and contributes to ocean acidification, which can harm organisms that build shells or skeletons.

Final answer

The enhanced greenhouse effect is a human-driven strengthening of a natural process. Reducing emissions and protecting carbon-storing ecosystems can limit its impacts.

Importance → five direct drivers → ecological/social consequences → conservation.

  1. Biodiversity is variety at genetic, species and ecosystem levels; it provides food, medicine, fibre, fuel, pollination, soil formation, water purification, climate regulation and cultural value.
  2. IPBES identifies five direct drivers: land- and sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species.
  3. Consequences include species decline or extinction, lost genetic diversity, and loss of pollinators, pest control, water purification and soil protection.
  4. Food production and fisheries may become less reliable; forest, wetland and coastal communities can lose income, food and cultural practices, and ecosystems become less resilient.

Biodiversity is the variety of life at genetic, species and ecosystem levels. It is essential because ecosystems provide food, medicine, fibre, fuel, pollination, soil formation, water purification, climate regulation and cultural value. Biodiversity also supports agriculture, fisheries, livelihoods and resilience to environmental change.

Major causes of biodiversity loss: IPBES identifies five major direct drivers: (1) land- and sea-use change, including deforestation and habitat fragmentation; (2) direct exploitation of organisms, such as overfishing, hunting and excessive logging; (3) climate change; (4) pollution of air, soil and water; and (5) invasive alien species that outcompete or prey on native species. Final answer

Biodiversity is not simply a collection of species; it underpins ecosystem services and human well-being. Conserving it is necessary for sustainable development.
