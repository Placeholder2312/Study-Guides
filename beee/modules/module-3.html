Fundamentals — what a transformer is and why we need one

1 · The problem it solves

Electrical power is generated at a few hundred volts, but it is used hundreds of kilometres away and at a safe voltage (230 V in India). Moving power along a wire always wastes some of it as heat, and that waste depends on the square of the current:

Ploss = I2Rline

For the same power, raising the voltage lowers the current (P = VI), and lowering the current cuts the line loss dramatically. So power is stepped up to a very high voltage for the journey, then stepped down again near homes and factories. A transformer is the device that does this, and it does it without any moving parts.

“Indeed, the transformer has made long-distance transmission of electric power a practical reality, as AC voltage can be ‘stepped up’ and current ‘stepped down’ for reduced wire resistance power losses along power lines connecting generating stations with loads.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9 “Transformers”, section “Step-up and step-down transformers”. Chapter 9.

Guide Before efficient transformers existed, the same job was done by a motor driving a generator. That arrangement has moving parts, wears out, and loses more energy. A transformer has no rotating parts at all, which is why its efficiency is so high — the point Dr. Nirmala’s slides make in §8.

2 · The definition to memorise

“A single phase transformer is a static device that is used to change the voltage of alternating current from one value to another without changing the frequency. It transfers power from one circuit to other without change in frequency.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 3 (“Transformer/single phase transformer”). Course slide deck.

Four words in that definition carry the whole chapter:

WordWhat it rules out
StaticNo rotating parts, no friction or windage loss — a major reason for 97–99% efficiency.
AlternatingIt does not work on steady DC. It needs a changing flux, and only AC produces one.
VoltageIt changes voltage and current levels. It does not change frequency, and it cannot create power.
Single phaseOne AC supply, one primary winding, one secondary winding. Three-phase transformers are a separate topic.

3 · The vocabulary you need before §4

Primary winding
The coil connected to the AC source. It takes power in. Its turns are counted as N1.
Secondary winding
The coil connected to the load. It delivers power out. Its turns are counted as N2.
Magnetic core
The iron path that carries the flux from one winding to the other, so that almost all of it links both coils.
Magnetic flux (Φ)
The magnetic “flow” in the core, in webers (Wb). It is produced by current in the primary.
Reluctance
Opposition of a magnetic path to flux — the magnetic analogue of resistance. Iron has low reluctance, air has high reluctance.
EMF
Electromotive force: the voltage induced in a winding by changing flux, in volts (V).
Turns (N)
How many times the wire is wound around the core. Turns decide the voltage, not the wire thickness.
Frequency (f)
How many cycles of AC per second, in hertz (Hz). In India, 50 Hz. A transformer does not alter it.

4 · Why a transformer needs AC

Induction depends on flux changing. An alternating current reverses direction 50 times a second, so the flux it creates in the core rises, collapses, reverses and rises again — continuously changing. A steady direct current produces a steady flux, and a steady flux induces no EMF at all.

“It operates only with alternating current (AC) because direct current (DC) does not create any electromagnetic induction.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 6 (“Construction of Single Phase Transformer”). Course slide deck.

Guide This is also a safety point. If you connect a transformer’s primary to DC, there is no back-EMF to oppose the supply; the current is limited only by the very small winding resistance, so it can become large enough to burn the winding. A transformer is an AC device, not a DC device.

Construction — core, windings and insulation

A single-phase transformer is two coils sharing one iron path. Everything else — tank, bushings, cooling — exists to protect and cool those three parts.

1 · The core

The core is built from thin sheets of silicon steel, stacked and insulated from each other. It does two jobs: it gives the flux an easy (low-reluctance) path, and it couples the two windings so that the same flux links both. Laminations matter: a solid iron block would let induced currents — eddy currents — circulate inside it and waste energy as heat (see §7).

“The core is made up of silicon steel laminations to reduce eddy current losses. The core provides a low reluctance path for the magnetic flux.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 6. Course slide deck.

2 · The windings

Two insulated copper coils are wound on the core. The primary is the input side, the secondary the output side. The number of turns is chosen for the required voltage; the wire thickness is chosen for the current the winding must carry.

“The number of turns in the primary winding is kept as minimum as possible to reduce copper losses. … The number of turns is adjusted to get the required output voltage value from the induced EMF.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 6. Course slide deck.

Guide A step-down transformer’s secondary carries more current than its primary, so its wire must be thicker. The thick, short secondary winding and the thin, long primary winding are a quick visual clue to which side is which — the point Kuphaldt makes with a cut-open transformer photograph.

3 · Insulation, mechanical parts and cooling

The windings must be insulated from the core and from each other, otherwise the “no direct electrical connection” property is lost and the voltage difference between the windings can flash over. Larger transformers add a steel tank of insulating oil (which both cools the windings and improves insulation), a conservator, cooling tubes or radiators, and porcelain bushings that bring the leads out safely. This paragraph is background; the syllabus only requires the core, the two windings and their function.

Working principle — mutual induction and the EMF equation

1 · The chain of events

  1. An alternating voltage V1 is applied to the primary winding.
  2. An alternating current I1 flows in the primary. Because the coil is wound on iron, a strong alternating magnetic flux Φ is set up in the core.
  3. That flux is confined to the core, so it links both windings — the secondary sees the same changing flux as the primary.
  4. By Faraday’s law, a changing flux induces an EMF in every winding it links. So an EMF E2 appears across the secondary, even though the secondary is not connected to the primary.
  5. When a load is connected, E2 drives a current I2 through the load. Energy has been transferred — magnetically, not by conduction.
  6. Taking more current from the secondary makes the primary draw more current from the source. The transformer is a load on the source and a source to the load.

“The effect is called mutual inductance: the induction of a voltage in one coil in response to a change in current in the other coil.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9, section “Mutual inductance and basic operation”. Chapter 9.

The teacher’s slides state the principle in one line: “The transformer works on the principle of mutual inductance between the two windings.” (Dr. Nirmala K., Unit 3 slides, slide 7.)

2 · Faraday’s law — the reason it works at all

The induced EMF depends on how fast the flux through a winding changes and on how many turns the winding has:

e = −N dΦ/dt

Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction. The minus sign comes from Lenz’s law: the induced EMF opposes the change that caused it.

The teacher’s slides introduce the topic with the same law: “Faraday’s law states that an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in any closed circuit when there is a change in the magnetic flux through the circuit.” (slide 3) Three consequences follow directly:

  • No change, no EMF. A steady flux (steady primary current) induces nothing. This is the whole reason transformers are AC devices.
  • Faster change, larger EMF. Higher frequency means faster flux change, so the EMF rises with frequency.
  • More turns, larger EMF. Two windings on the same core see the same dΦ/dt, so their EMFs are in proportion to their turns. That single sentence is the transformation ratio.

3 · The EMF equation

For a sinusoidal supply of frequency f, with a maximum flux Φm in the core, the RMS EMF induced in a winding of N turns is:

E = 4.44 f N Φm

E in volts, f in hertz, N dimensionless, Φm in webers. The constant 4.44 = 2π/√2 comes from converting a sinusoidal peak value to an RMS value.

Where 4.44 comes from — derivation outline

Take the flux as sinusoidal: Φ = Φm sin ωt, with ω = 2πf. Differentiate:

e = −N dΦ/dt = −N Φm ω cos ωt

So the peak EMF is Emax = N Φm ω = 2πfNΦm. For a sine wave the RMS value is the peak divided by √2:

E = 2πfNΦm / √2 = 4.44 f N Φm

The same derivation, with the same result, appears in the teacher’s slides (slides 7–9). The exam question of June 2025 asks for exactly this: state the principle, then derive the equation. Write the steps, not just the final line.

Note on signs: the slides write E = −N dΦ/dt for the instantaneous EMF and drop the minus sign when converting to a magnitude. Either is acceptable in an answer as long as you say which one you are writing.

Applying the equation to both windings of the same core, with the same f and the same Φm:

E1 / N1 = E2 / N2 = 4.44 f Φm

“Volts per turn” is the same in both windings. This is the seed of the transformation ratio in §5.

One illustration — what the equation means, not a numerical exercise

Suppose a transformer has 500 primary turns and 1 000 secondary turns on the same core, and the primary is connected to an AC supply. Since the secondary has twice as many turns, it gets twice the volts per turn, so the secondary voltage is twice the primary voltage — a step-up transformer. The current moves the other way: twice the voltage at the same power means half the current. Nothing here needs a calculator; the syllabus expects you to be able to say why the voltage doubles and the current halves.

4 · Two details students often get wrong

  • The secondary is not “powered by the primary’s current” in the obvious sense. It is powered by the flux. Even with the secondary open, the primary draws a small magnetising current to establish the flux; the moment a load draws current, the primary current rises to supply that energy.
  • Flux, voltage and current have phase relationships. In a lossless inductor the flux lags the applied voltage by 90°, which is why the no-load primary current is mainly magnetising current. This is beyond what the syllabus asks, but it explains why the no-load current is not zero even when nothing is connected.

Types of single-phase transformers

“Types” in this module means classification, not one single list. The same transformer can be described by its voltage function, by how its core is built, by how it is cooled, and by what it is used for. Learn one table and you can place any transformer in it.

1 · Step-up and step-down

A step-up transformer has more secondary turns than primary turns, so it raises the voltage. A step-down transformer has fewer secondary turns, so it lowers the voltage. In both cases the current changes in the opposite direction, so the power is (almost) unchanged.

“A transformer that increases voltage from primary to secondary (more secondary winding turns than primary winding turns) is called a step-up transformer. Conversely, a transformer designed to do just the opposite is called a step-down transformer.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9, section “Step-up and step-down transformers”. Chapter 9.

Guide A transformer can physically be used backwards — a step-up unit can step down if you feed the secondary. But each winding is designed for a particular voltage and current, so using it outside those ratings is inefficient and can overheat it. Treat the label as the design direction.

2 · Core type and shell type

In a core-type transformer the windings surround the core limbs: the magnetic path is a closed rectangle and the coils sit on it (Figure 1). In a shell-type transformer the core surrounds the windings, giving the flux two parallel return paths; the core is effectively a shell around the coils. 3 · Berry (toroidal) transformer

“Berry transformer / toroidal transformer … has been widely used in home appliances and other electronic equipment with high technical requirements. Its main use is as a power transformer and an isolation transformer. Widely used in computers, medical equipment, telecommunications, instruments and lighting.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 15 (“Berry type transformer”). Course slide deck.

The toroidal shape keeps the flux path short and closed, so it is compact, has low leakage and produces little external magnetic field — useful in medical and audio equipment.

4 · Isolation transformer

An isolation transformer has a 1:1 turns ratio: the secondary voltage equals the primary voltage, but the two circuits are still separated by the core. Its purpose is safety and separation, not voltage change.

“Transformers designed to provide electrical isolation without stepping voltage and current either up or down are called isolation transformers.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9, section “Electrical isolation”. Chapter 9.

5 · Autotransformer

An autotransformer uses a single tapped winding: part of the winding is shared by the primary and secondary circuits. It needs less copper and is smaller and cheaper, but it gives no electrical isolation — a fault on one side can reach the other. Variacs (variable autotransformers) use a sliding contact and are common in laboratories.

6 · Instrument transformers

Voltage and current in power systems are far too large for meters to handle directly. A potential transformer steps voltage down to a few tens of volts for a voltmeter; a current transformer steps current down to about 5 A for an ammeter. Both are single-phase transformers used for measurement and protection, not for supplying power.

Transformation ratio

The transformation ratio is the single number that tells you what a transformer does to voltage and current. It comes straight from “same volts per turn”:

K = N2 / N1 = V2 / V1 = E2 / E1 = I1 / I2

This guide uses K = secondary ÷ primary, throughout, in every section and in the answer key. The teacher’s slides use the same convention: K = N2/N1.

One convention, stated once. Many textbooks call N1/N2 the ratio, or use the letter a. Both conventions are correct; mixing them is not. If your answer sheet uses a = N1/N2, everything is simply inverted, and step-up becomes a < 1. Write your definition at the top of the answer and stay with it.

1 · Where each part of the ratio comes from

  • Voltage and turns. Both windings see the same flux and frequency, so E ∝ N. Therefore E2/E1 = N2/N1. Neglecting the small drop across the winding resistance, the terminal voltages V1 and V2 are practically equal to E1 and E2, which is why the slides write V1 ≈ E1 and V2 ≈ E2.
  • Current and turns. For an ideal transformer the input power equals the output power, V1I1 = V2I2. Rearranging gives I1/I2 = V2/V1 — the current ratio is the inverse of the voltage ratio.
  • Why it cannot be otherwise. If the secondary delivered more power than the primary took in, energy would be created. A transformer only converts, never generates.
Value of K = N2/N1NameVoltageCurrentTypical use
K > 1Step-upV2 > V1I2 < I1At the generating station, before transmission
K < 1Step-downV2 < V1I2 > I1Substation and local distribution, chargers, adaptors
K = 1Isolation (1:1)V2 = V1I2 = I1Safety isolation, test benches, medical equipment

Guide A useful sanity check for any answer: multiply voltage by current on each side. The two products should be nearly equal in a real transformer and exactly equal in an ideal one. If your numbers show the secondary with both higher voltage and higher current, something is wrong.

Ideal and practical transformers

An ideal transformer is a model: a set of simplifying assumptions that makes the arithmetic clean. A practical transformer is the real device, which breaks every one of those assumptions a little — and that is exactly where losses, heating and voltage drop come from.

1 · The five assumptions of an ideal transformer

  1. No winding resistance. The copper coils have zero resistance, so no I2R heat and no voltage drop inside the windings.
  2. No leakage flux. Every line of flux set up by the primary links the secondary; none escapes into the air.
  3. No core loss. The core needs no energy to magnetise and reverse magnetisation, and no eddy currents circulate in it.
  4. Infinite permeability. A tiny magnetising current is enough to establish the flux, so the no-load current is effectively zero.
  5. Therefore no losses at all: input power equals output power, V1I1 = V2I2, and efficiency is 100%.

“An ideal transformer would have perfect coupling (no leakage inductance), perfect voltage regulation, perfectly sinusoidal exciting current, no hysteresis or eddy current losses, and wire thick enough to handle any amount of current. Unfortunately, the ideal transformer would have to be infinitely large and heavy to meet these design goals.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9, section “Power capacity”. Chapter 9.

2 · What is different in a practical transformer

  • Winding resistance. Copper is a good conductor, not a perfect one. Current through it causes copper loss and a small internal voltage drop, so the terminal voltage sags as load current rises.
  • Leakage flux. Not quite all the flux links both windings; the part that links only one behaves like a small series inductance and worsens the voltage drop.
  • Finite permeability. Some magnetising current is always needed, so even an unloaded transformer draws a small current from the supply.
  • Core loss. Reversing the magnetisation each cycle (hysteresis) and induced currents inside the iron (eddy currents) both consume energy and heat the core.
  • Result. Output power is always a little less than input power; the difference leaves as heat. Efficiency is high, but never 100%.
  • Practical touches. Insulation has a limit, the core can saturate, and heat must be removed by air or oil. These are design limits rather than “losses”, but they are why real transformers are rated in kVA and must be operated within it.

4 · Voltage regulation — a practical consequence

Because of winding resistance and leakage, the secondary voltage of a real transformer drops as the load draws more current. Voltage regulation measures how well the transformer holds its secondary voltage between no load and full load; a small regulation figure is better. This is why a heavily loaded transformer’s output is a little below its rated voltage. Supplementary context — not a syllabus heading, but it explains the “practical” column above.

Transformer losses

Losses are the energy that enters the primary but never reaches the load. They all end up as heat, and they are grouped into two families by one question: does the loss depend on the load?

FamilyWhat causes itWhereDepends on load?Reduced by
Iron loss (core loss) PiHysteresis + eddy currentsMagnetic coreNo — practically constant at a given voltage and frequencySilicon-steel alloy, thin insulated laminations, low flux density
Copper loss PcuI2R heating in the windingsPrimary and secondary coilsYes — rises with the square of the load currentThicker wire, fewer turns, good joints, adequate cooling

So a transformer is never “lossless”, but the losses are small compared with the power handled, and they split neatly:

Ploss = Pi + Pcu   ·   Pcu = I2R

Pi is also written Wi and Pcu as Wcu in the teacher’s slides. Both notations are common.

1 · Copper loss

Every winding has resistance, so current through it dissipates I2R as heat. This is called copper loss (or I2R loss, or variable loss) because it changes with load: at half the load current it falls to one quarter. Because it is load-dependent, it is the loss you can influence by how hard you drive the transformer.

2 · Eddy-current loss

The core is itself a conductor sitting in an alternating magnetic field. By Faraday’s law, EMFs are induced inside the iron, and these drive small circulating currents — eddy currents — through the core’s own resistance, heating it. The teacher’s slide gives the relation you should be able to recognise:

We = Ke f2 Bm2 t2 V

f supply frequency, Bm maximum flux density, t lamination thickness, V core volume, Ke a material constant. Course Dr. Nirmala K., Unit 3 slides, photographed page on eddy-current loss.

The t2 dependence is why the core is laminated: halving the sheet thickness cuts eddy-current loss to about a quarter. Each lamination is coated with insulating varnish so the current cannot cross from sheet to sheet.

“Dividing the iron core into thin insulated laminations minimizes eddy current loss.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume II — AC, Chapter 9, section “Energy losses”. Chapter 9.

3 · Hysteresis loss

Iron does not give up its magnetism instantly; it “lags” behind the magnetising force. Every time the AC reverses, energy must be spent dragging the core’s magnetisation around its hysteresis loop — twice per cycle. That energy is hysteresis loss. It is reduced by choosing a magnetic material with a narrow loop (silicon steel is used for exactly this reason) and by keeping the flux density low, which means a generous core area.

Wh = kh f Bm1.6 V

Steinmetz form. The slides write the constant as η; here it is kh to avoid confusion with efficiency. Note the loss rises with frequency, but not with the square of frequency as eddy loss does.

4 · Why iron loss is called the constant loss

Flux in the core is set by the applied voltage and frequency, not by the load. As long as the supply stays at its rated voltage and frequency, the core cycles through the same magnetisation pattern whether the secondary is open or fully loaded. So the iron loss stays essentially constant, while copper loss grows with load. This single fact is the backbone of efficiency calculations and of the load-dependence of efficiency.

5 · Finding the losses in practice — OC and SC tests

You cannot easily separate the two losses by simply loading a transformer, because the input and output measurements include everything at once. Instead:

  • Open-circuit (no-load) test: the secondary is left open, so almost no current flows and copper loss is negligible. What the primary draws is essentially the iron loss.
  • Short-circuit test: the secondary is shorted and a small primary voltage applied, so the core flux — and therefore the iron loss — is very small. What is measured is essentially the copper loss at that current.

Course Dr. Nirmala K.’s slides note that efficiency is found in practice from these two tests rather than from direct loading, which “may give wrong results”. Full test procedure is outside this module’s syllabus.

Efficiency

Efficiency compares what you get out with what you put in. For a transformer it is high — the teacher’s slides give 97% to 99% — because there are no moving parts, so there is no friction or windage loss.

“The ratio of output power to input power is known as transformer efficiency. … Transformer efficiency is measured in terms of percentages, it ranges from 97% to 99%. The transformer has no moving parts so the losses due to friction and windage are absent therefore its efficiency is very high.”

Dr. Nirmala K., ESC 102 BEEE Unit 3 CSE AIML, slide 21 (“Efficiency of transformer”). Course slide deck.
η = (Pout / Pin) × 100%

Since the input must cover the output plus every loss, the same definition can be written entirely in terms of losses:

η = Pout / (Pout + Pi + Pcu) × 100%

This form is the more useful one: it shows exactly where the missing power went.

1 · Output power and power factor

The output is AC, so the actual power delivered depends on the load’s power factor:

Pout = V2 I2 cos φ

For a resistive load cos φ = 1. For motors and other inductive loads it is less than 1, so the same voltage and current deliver less real power. This is why a transformer is rated in kVA rather than kW: kVA is the product of rated voltage and rated current, and it does not depend on what power factor the customer’s load happens to have.

2 · How efficiency changes with load

Let x be the fraction of full load (so x = 0.5 at half load). Copper loss scales with the square of current, while iron loss stays essentially constant:

Pcu at load x = x2 × Pcu,full load   ·   Pi = constant

Three consequences follow, and they are the ones examiners ask about:

  • At very light load the output is small but the iron loss is already being paid in full, so efficiency is low.
  • As load increases the output grows faster than the losses, so efficiency rises.
  • Beyond a certain point the copper loss (growing as the square of current) takes over and efficiency begins to fall again.

Supplementary The peak occurs where copper loss equals iron loss. Distribution transformers are deliberately designed so this peak falls near their normal daily load, which is why they run near their best efficiency most of the day.

3 · Why we do not load a transformer to 100% continuously

Efficiency is only part of the story: heat removal sets the real limit. A transformer rated 100 kVA can deliver 100 kVA, but the resulting copper loss must be carried away by its cooling. Continuous overload shortens insulation life. That is why ratings are conservative and why oil-filled units have radiators.

4 · All-day efficiency

A distribution transformer sits energised all day but is only heavily loaded for part of it. Its all-day (energy) efficiency is the energy delivered over 24 hours divided by the energy supplied over 24 hours. Because the iron loss runs for all 24 hours, a unit with low iron loss scores better on this measure than one chosen purely for peak-load efficiency.

Applications worth adding to the list

  • Power supplies and rectifiers. A step-down transformer followed by a rectifier turns mains AC into the low DC that electronics need — the example on slide 24. The transformer also isolates the low-voltage side from the mains.
  • Measurement and protection. Potential and current transformers scale high voltages and currents down to meter-safe values in substations.
  • Welding. A step-down transformer supplies the very large currents welding needs at a low, safe open-circuit voltage.
  • Audio, medical and instrumentation equipment. Toroidal (berry-type) transformers are used where low leakage and compact size matter.
  • Laboratory work. Variacs (variable autotransformers) give a smoothly adjustable AC voltage for experiments.

One limitation to remember. A transformer changes voltage and current, and it can isolate two circuits — but it cannot change frequency and it cannot convert AC to DC by itself. Rectification needs diodes; frequency conversion needs electronics. Saying this in an answer shows you understand what the device actually does.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming a transformer changes frequency. It does not — only voltage and current.
  • Saying the secondary is “connected” to the primary. It is magnetically coupled, electrically isolated.
  • Writing I2/I1 = N2/N1. The current ratio is the inverse of the voltage ratio.
  • Calling iron loss “variable” and copper loss “constant”. It is the other way round.
  • Saying efficiency is 100% in a practical transformer. It is high, not perfect.
  • Listing eddy-current loss as a copper loss. It occurs in the core, so it is an iron loss.
  • Forgetting that the core is laminated to reduce eddy currents, not to reduce hysteresis.
  • Claiming a transformer works on DC. It needs a changing flux.

Answer key

These are model answers written for this page, not official university solutions. Marks are not assigned because the questions are not from a paper.

1 · Definition

A single-phase transformer is a static device that transfers electrical energy between two magnetically coupled windings by mutual induction, changing the voltage of an alternating supply from one level to another. It does not change the frequency, and it does not change the power (apart from losses).

2 · Three essential parts

Core — a laminated silicon-steel path that carries the flux and links the windings with low reluctance. Primary winding — connected to the AC supply, sets up the flux. Secondary winding — connected to the load, in which the EMF is induced.

3 · Lamination

The core is built from thin, varnish-insulated sheets so that eddy currents induced in the iron cannot circulate over a large area. Since eddy-current loss depends on the square of the lamination thickness, thin sheets cut the loss sharply. Lamination reduces eddy-current loss; hysteresis loss is reduced by material choice, not by laminating.

4 · Faraday’s law and AC

e = −N dΦ/dt: an EMF is induced in a coil only while the flux linking it changes. An alternating supply produces a continuously changing flux, so an EMF is continuously induced. A steady DC produces a steady flux; dΦ/dt = 0, so no EMF appears in the secondary. Hence transformers work only on AC.

5 · EMF equation

E = 4.44 f N Φm, where E is the RMS induced EMF (V), f the supply frequency (Hz), N the number of turns in the winding, and Φm the maximum flux in the core (Wb).

6 · Working principle

An alternating voltage applied to the primary drives an alternating primary current, which establishes an alternating flux in the laminated core. The core confines the flux so that it links both windings. By Faraday’s law, the changing flux induces an EMF in the secondary; with a load connected, that EMF drives a secondary current and energy is delivered to the load. The primary draws extra current in response. Energy therefore travels as electrical → magnetic → electrical, and the two windings need no electrical connection. The principle is mutual induction. (Draw Figure 2 and label the supply, primary, core, flux, secondary and load.)

7 · Derivation of the EMF equation

Let Φ = Φm sin ωt with ω = 2πf. Then

e = −N dΦ/dt = −NΦmω cos ωt

so Emax = NΦmω = 2πfNΦm. For a sine wave, RMS = peak/√2:

E = 2πfNΦm/√2 = 4.44 f N Φm

Applying this to both windings gives E1/N1 = E2/N2.

8 · Losses

Two families. Iron (core) loss: hysteresis loss, from repeatedly reversing the core’s magnetisation, plus eddy-current loss, from circulating currents induced in the iron. Both are practically constant at rated voltage and frequency, and both are reduced by using silicon steel and thin laminations with low flux density. Copper loss: I2R heating in the primary and secondary windings; it varies with the square of the load current and is reduced by using thicker conductors. Total loss = iron loss + copper loss.

9 · Ideal versus practical

(i) Winding resistance: zero in the ideal case, finite in practice, causing copper loss and voltage drop. (ii) Leakage: none ideally, some in practice, worsening regulation. (iii) Core: lossless and infinitely permeable ideally, with hysteresis and eddy losses in practice. (iv) No-load current: zero ideally, a small magnetising current in practice. (v) Efficiency: 100% ideally, typically 97–99% in practice. (Any four points, each with the consequence, earns full credit.)

10 · Transformation ratio

With K = N2/N1: both windings see the same flux and frequency, so EMF is proportional to turns and V2/V1 ≈ N2/N1. For an ideal transformer V1I1 = V2I2, hence I1/I2 = V2/V1 = K. So K = N2/N1 = V2/V1 = I1/I2. Step-up: K > 1. Step-down: K < 1. Isolation: K = 1. (State your convention explicitly: if you use a = N1/N2, the inequalities invert.)

11 · Core type versus shell type

(i) In core type the windings surround the core limbs; in shell type the core surrounds the windings. (ii) Core type is used for distribution duty, shell type for supplying power when required. (iii) Core type is in service throughout the day; shell type is used when power is needed. (iv) The slide table gives lower efficiency for core type and 90–98% for shell type. (v) Core type is bulkier and costlier; shell type is compact, cheaper and portable. (vi) Core type is associated with electricity boards and large installations; shell type with individual customers such as welding shops.

12 · Efficiency

η = (Pout/Pin) × 100% = Pout/(Pout + Pi + Pcu) × 100%. It is very high (97–99%) because the device is static: there is no friction or windage loss, and copper and iron losses are small at rated design. At very light load the output is small while the iron loss is still being supplied in full, so the ratio of losses to output is large and efficiency is low. As load rises, copper loss grows as x2 while output grows as x, so efficiency rises to a maximum and then falls.

13 · A 230 V / 12 V transformer

It is a step-down transformer (K = 12/230 ≈ 0.052 < 1). The 12 V winding has far fewer turns. Because it supplies the higher current, it is wound with thicker wire; the 230 V primary, carrying less current, uses thinner wire.

14 · Frequency and the two core losses

Hysteresis loss is proportional to frequency because the core is taken around its hysteresis loop once per cycle, so more cycles per second means more energy per second (Wh ∝ f). Eddy-current loss rises with the square of frequency, because the induced EMF in the core rises with f and the resulting current, multiplied by voltage again, gives an f2 dependence. Hence high-frequency transformers need thinner laminations (or ferrite/powdered-iron cores).

15 · The “more power” error

The secondary has a higher voltage but a correspondingly lower current. In an ideal transformer V1I1 = V2I2, so the power is unchanged; in a practical one the output is slightly less than the input. If a step-up transformer produced more power than it received, energy would be created, violating conservation of energy. A transformer converts voltage and current levels; it does not generate power.

16 · kVA rating

The heating of a transformer depends on its voltage (which sets the flux and hence iron loss) and its current (which sets copper loss). Neither depends on the phase angle of the customer’s load. Rating in kVA therefore states the voltage and current the insulation and cooling can tolerate, leaving the actual kW delivered to be determined by the load’s power factor.

17 · Unloaded transformer

A small current still flows because the primary must establish the alternating flux in the core — the magnetising current — and must supply hysteresis and eddy-current losses. So the primary behaves like an iron-cored inductor, not an open circuit. It becomes slightly warm because those core losses are dissipated as heat inside the iron even with the secondary open.

18 · Maximum efficiency below full load

Efficiency peaks where copper loss equals iron loss. Since copper loss at full load is deliberately made larger than the iron loss, the equality point falls at a fraction of full load (typically around 70–85%). A distribution transformer spends most of the day at part load, so putting the peak there maximises the energy it delivers for the energy it consumes.

19 · Phone charger

It is a small single-phase step-down transformer (in modern chargers, a high-frequency switched-mode transformer). It does provide isolation: the low-voltage side is not electrically connected to the mains. After the transformer the low AC is rectified to DC, smoothed by a capacitor and regulated to the required output — the transformer itself cannot produce DC.
