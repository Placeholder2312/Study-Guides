Prerequisites — from charge to power

DC circuit analysis is only six ideas repeated: charge, current, voltage, resistance, power and the two ways of connecting resistors. If one of these is shaky, every later method will feel like a trick. This section builds them in order and ends with one complete series–parallel example.

1 · Charge and current

Electric charge is measured in coulombs (C). The symbol is Q. Current is the rate at which charge passes a point:

I = Q / t   and   1 A = 1 C/s

If 12 C pass a point in 3 s, the current is 4 A. Conventional current is drawn from the positive terminal of a source, through the external circuit, to the negative terminal. Electrons drift the other way. All the arrow rules in this module use conventional current.

Direct current (DC) means the current does not reverse. Its value may still change with time, but in this module the sources are constant, so every current and voltage settles to a fixed value.

2 · Voltage is a difference

Voltage, or potential difference, is the work done per unit charge in moving charge between two points:

V = W / Q   and   1 V = 1 J/C

A voltage does not exist “at a point” by itself. It exists between two points. When we say “the voltage at node A is 6 V,” we always mean “6 V at A relative to the reference node.” The reference node is usually drawn at the bottom of the circuit and called ground (0 V). Choosing a different reference shifts every node voltage by the same amount; every difference, and therefore every current, stays the same.

The plus and minus signs on a voltage tell you which point is at the higher potential. VAB = VA − VB is positive when A is higher than B. Reversing the order reverses the sign.

3 · Resistance and Ohm’s law

Resistance is opposition to current, measured in ohms (Ω). For a resistor at constant temperature:

V = I R   ·   I = V / R   ·   R = V / I

Conductance is the reciprocal, G = 1/R, measured in siemens (S). Nodal analysis becomes faster if you think in conductances, because a resistor R between two nodes contributes G = 1/R to the equations.

An ideal wire has zero resistance: all points on it are at the same potential. An open circuit has infinite resistance: no current flows. A short circuit has zero resistance: the voltage across it is zero, and the current is limited only by the rest of the circuit.

4 · Series and parallel

Series: the same current passes through every element. The voltages add.

Rseries = R1 + R2 + ⋯ + Rn

Parallel: the same voltage appears across every element. The currents add.

1 / Rparallel = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ⋯ + 1/Rn   ·   two resistors: Rp = R1R2 / (R1 + R2)

For two resistors, “product over sum” is faster than the reciprocal formula. The parallel equivalent is always smaller than the smallest branch.

Worked example — reduce, then work backwards

Given. A 12 V ideal source feeds 6 Ω in series with the parallel combination of 3 Ω and 6 Ω (Figure 1).

Required. Total current, the voltage at A, and the current in each parallel branch.

Step 1 — parallel pair.

Rp = (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) = 18/9 = 2 Ω

Step 2 — total resistance.

Rtotal = 6 + 2 = 8 Ω

Step 3 — source current.

I = 12/8 = 1.5 A

Step 4 — voltage at A. The 6 Ω series resistor drops V = 1.5 × 6 = 9 V, so VA = 12 − 9 = 3 V. Equivalently, VA = 1.5 × 2 = 3 V.

Step 5 — branch currents.

I3Ω = 3/3 = 1 A   ·   I6Ω = 3/6 = 0.5 A

Check. 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 A, which is the source current. Power: source supplies 12 × 1.5 = 18 W; resistors dissipate 1.5²×6 + 1²×3 + 0.5²×6 = 13.5 + 3 + 1.5 = 18 W. Balances.

5 · Power and energy

P = V I = I²R = V²/R   ·   E = P t

Power is in watts (W), energy in joules (J). A 100 W lamp running for 10 h uses 1 kWh, commonly called one “unit” of electricity. In circuit problems, power is the best check: the power delivered by sources must equal the power absorbed by resistors (and by any source that is being charged).

Passive sign convention. If current enters the + terminal of an element, that element absorbs power: P = V I is positive. If current leaves the + terminal, the element delivers power. A resistor always absorbs; a source may deliver or absorb.

6 · Algebra and units you will actually use

  • Simultaneous equations. Mesh and nodal analysis produce two or three linear equations. Solve by substitution or elimination; keep the units on every term.
  • Fractions are normal. Answers such as 41/48 A are exact. Convert to decimals only at the end, and state the unit.
  • Prefixes. k = 10³, m = 10⁻³, μ = 10⁻⁶. Convert every value to base units before calculating.
  • Signs carry meaning. A negative current means the actual current is opposite to your assumed arrow. Do not throw the minus sign away.

7 · What “source” means here

An independent source sets a voltage or current to a fixed value regardless of the rest of the circuit. A dependent source’s value depends on another voltage or current in the circuit. The syllabus says “only independent sources,” so every source in this module is independent. You will meet dependent sources in a later module; do not use dependent-source techniques here.

The syllabus page also lists resistance, inductance, capacitance, series/parallel resistance, voltage/current/power/energy, and magnetic circuits (MMF, field strength, reluctance). Inductance, capacitance and magnetic circuits are not used in Module I’s DC-resistive analysis. They become essential in later modules. Reference node and node voltage

Pick one node and call it 0 V. Every other node voltage is then measured with respect to it. The choice is free, but choosing the bottom rail or the negative terminal of the main source usually makes the equations shortest. A node voltage is always a rise from the reference to that node.

Assumed directions

You do not need to know the true direction before solving. Draw an arrow, solve, and interpret the sign:

  • Positive answer → the current really flows in the direction of your arrow.
  • Negative answer → the current flows opposite to your arrow; the magnitude is the absolute value.

The same applies to voltage polarity. Mark + and − before writing equations, and keep them consistent to the end.

Two assumptions that are always in force

  • Ideal wires. Connecting wires have zero resistance unless a value is printed. All points on a wire are at one potential.
  • Lumped elements. Each resistor, source and meter is treated as a single element at a point. There are no stray capacitances or inductances in this module.

Ideal and practical sources

A source is a two-terminal device that maintains a voltage or a current. “Ideal” and “practical” are models, not moral judgements: the ideal model is simpler, the practical model is more accurate.

“An ideal battery is an emf source that maintains a constant terminal voltage, independent of the current between the two terminals. An ideal battery has no internal resistance…”

Samuel J. Ling, William Moebs and Jeff Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, OpenStax, §10.1 “Electromotive Force”. Section link. CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

Paraphrase. An ideal voltage source holds its terminal voltage at Vs whatever current the rest of the circuit draws. Its V–I graph is a vertical line at V = Vs. An ideal current source holds its current at Is whatever voltage appears across it; its V–I graph is a horizontal line at I = Is. Neither can be built exactly, but they are excellent approximations when the internal resistance is very small (voltage source) or very large (current source).

A real battery has internal resistance. OpenStax models it as an ideal emf in series with a resistance:

“A simple model for a battery consists of an idealized emf source ε and an internal resistance r.”

“The terminal voltage of the battery is Vterminal = ε − I r.”

OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §10.1, Figure 10.6 and Equation 10.1. Section link.
Worked example — terminal voltage of a real battery

Given. ε = 12 V, internal resistance r = 0.5 Ω, load current I = 4 A.

Required. Terminal voltage and the power lost inside the battery.

Equation.

Vterminal = ε − I r = 12 − (4)(0.5)

Solve. Vterminal = 12 − 2 = 10 V. Internal loss = I²r = 16 × 0.5 = 8 W.

Check. The load receives 10 × 4 = 40 W; the battery chemically supplies 40 + 8 = 48 W = 12 × 4. Balances.

When to use the practical model. Use it whenever a problem gives an internal resistance, asks for terminal voltage under load, or asks why a battery’s voltage falls when a heavy load is connected. Use the ideal model when no internal resistance is given and the source is drawn as an ideal symbol.

Common mistakes — sources

  • Treating an ideal voltage source as if it had a fixed current. Its current is set by the external circuit.
  • Forgetting that an ideal current source has a voltage set by the external circuit, not by the source alone.
  • Putting the internal resistance in parallel with a voltage source. It is in series.
  • Putting the internal resistance in series with a current source. It is in parallel.
  • Using Vterminal = ε + I r while the battery is discharging. The sign is minus when current leaves the positive terminal.

Source transformation

Source transformation replaces a voltage source in series with a resistance by a current source in parallel with the same resistance, or the reverse. It is not a new law; it is a way of redrawing the same two-terminal behaviour.

Vs = Is Rp   ·   Is = Vs / Rs   ·   Rp = Rs

The resistance value does not change; it moves from series to parallel. The source value changes so that the short-circuit current and open-circuit voltage stay the same.

Why it works

Look at the two models from the terminals. For the voltage model, the short-circuit current is Vs/Rs and the open-circuit voltage is Vs. For the current model, the short-circuit current is Is and the open-circuit voltage is IsRp. If Is = Vs/Rs and Rp = Rs, the two terminal behaviours are identical for every external load. That is the definition of equivalence.

Worked example — transform, combine, then find the current

Given. An 18 V source in series with 6 Ω feeds a 3 Ω resistor connected across the output.

Required. The current in the 3 Ω resistor.

Step 1 — transform the source. 18 V in series with 6 Ω becomes 3 A in parallel with 6 Ω.

Step 2 — combine the parallel resistors. The 6 Ω and 3 Ω are now in parallel:

Rp = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 Ω

Step 3 — one current source, one resistor. 3 A flows into 2 Ω, so

V = 3 × 2 = 6 V

Step 4 — go back to the original branch. The 3 Ω resistor has 6 V across it:

I3Ω = 6/3 = 2 A

Check. Original circuit: total resistance 6 + 3 = 9 Ω, source current 18/9 = 2 A; that current all passes through the 3 Ω resistor. Matches.

Rules and limits

  • The resistance keeps the same numerical value; only its position changes.
  • Keep the terminal polarity consistent. The current source arrow points to the terminal that was the + terminal of the voltage source.
  • Transform only the part of the circuit you are allowed to change. The load is not part of the source model.
  • An ideal voltage source with Rs = 0 cannot be transformed (it would need an infinite current source). An ideal current source with Rp = ∞ cannot be transformed.
  • Source transformation works for independent sources. Dependent sources would need extra care and are outside this syllabus.

Common mistakes — source transformation

  • Putting the resistance in parallel after converting to a voltage source (or in series after converting to a current source). The position reverses.
  • Reversing the arrow. The arrow head must point to the + terminal of the equivalent voltage source.
  • Changing the resistance value as well as the source value. R stays the same.
  • Transforming a source that is in series with another source without first combining them.
  • Using the transformed circuit to find power in the original internal resistance. The transformation preserves terminal behaviour, not the internal power distribution.

Kirchhoff’s laws (KCL and KVL)

Kirchhoff’s two laws turn a circuit into a set of equations. KCL is charge conservation at a junction; KVL is energy conservation around a loop. Every method in the rest of this module is a shortcut built on these two laws.

“The sum of all currents entering a junction must equal the sum of all currents leaving the junction.”

OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §10.3 “Kirchhoff’s Rules”, Kirchhoff’s first rule (the junction rule). Section link.

“The algebraic sum of changes in potential around any closed circuit path (loop) must be zero.”

OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §10.3, Kirchhoff’s second rule (the loop rule). Section link.

Paraphrase. KCL says that charge cannot pile up at a node in a steady-state circuit: whatever enters must leave. KVL says that if you walk around any closed loop and add every potential rise and drop with its sign, you return to the same potential, so the sum is zero.

KCL — the junction rule

At a node, write either “sum of currents in = sum of currents out” or “sum of currents leaving = 0.” The second form is usually better for nodal analysis because every term can be written as (node voltage − neighbour voltage)/resistance.

Σ Iin = Σ Iout   or   Σ Ileaving = 0

A node with only two connections does not need a KCL equation: the same current flows in and out. KCL becomes useful at a junction of three or more branches.

KVL — the loop rule

Choose a direction around the loop (clockwise is conventional). Walk the loop and add each element voltage using the sign table in the conventions section. The sum is zero.

Σ Vrises = Σ Vdrops   or   Σ ΔV = 0

A resistor always drops potential in the direction of its current. A source rises potential when you go from − to + and drops when you go from + to −.

Worked example — the same circuit by KCL and by KVL

Given. 10 V ideal source, 2 Ω series resistor, node A, then 2 Ω and 4 Ω in parallel to the bottom rail (Figure 4).

Required. VA, the current in each resistor, and a power check.

Assumed directions. Current leaves the + terminal of the 10 V source, flows right through the 2 Ω series resistor into A, then down through the 2 Ω and 4 Ω resistors.

KCL at A. Take the bottom rail as 0 V. Current entering A from the source is (10 − VA)/2. Currents leaving are VA/2 and VA/4.

(10 − VA)/2 = VA/2 + VA/4

Solve. Multiply by 4:

20 − 2VA = 2VA + VA  →  20 = 5VA  →  VA = 4 V

Branch currents. Source branch: (10 − 4)/2 = 3 A. 2 Ω branch: 4/2 = 2 A. 4 Ω branch: 4/4 = 1 A.

KVL check, left mesh. Start at the bottom-left corner, go clockwise: +10 − 2(3) − 2(2) = 10 − 6 − 4 = 0. ✓

KVL check, right mesh. +2(2) − 4(1) = 4 − 4 = 0. ✓

Power check. Source supplies 10 × 3 = 30 W. Resistors absorb 3²×2 + 2²×2 + 1²×4 = 18 + 8 + 4 = 30 W. ✓

Answer. VA = 4 V; 3 A from the source, 2 A down the 2 Ω branch, 1 A down the 4 Ω branch.

When a source fixes a quantity

  • Voltage source between a node and the reference: that node voltage is known immediately. Do not write a KCL equation for it; use KCL at the other nodes and find the source current last.
  • Current source in a branch: that branch current is known. Use it as a term in KCL and find the voltage across the source last.

If a voltage source sits between two non-reference nodes, the strict method needs a supernode. That is explicitly outside this syllabus. Choose the reference at one end of the source, or use source transformation, so the problem stays within scope.

Exam procedure — KCL/KVL

  1. Redraw the circuit and mark every node. Choose the bottom rail (or the negative terminal of the main source) as 0 V.
  2. Mark assumed current directions and label them I1, I2, ….
  3. Write KCL at the nodes that have three or more connections. Do not write redundant equations for every node.
  4. Write KVL for enough independent loops to cover every unknown. Two unknowns need two independent equations.
  5. Solve the simultaneous equations. Keep fractions exact until the final decimal.
  6. Check one loop and the power balance. State the direction of every current, not just its magnitude.

Common mistakes — KCL/KVL

  • Using the wrong sign for a resistor: a drop is negative when you travel with the current.
  • Forgetting a branch at a node, especially a source branch. Every wire connected to the node counts.
  • Writing KCL at the reference node and at every other node, then wondering why one equation is redundant. One node equation is always dependent.
  • Treating a mesh current as if it were the current in every branch of that mesh. Shared branches carry the difference of two mesh currents.
  • Dropping the units and adding volts to amperes.
  • Reporting a negative answer as “wrong.” The negative sign is the direction.

Mesh analysis

Mesh analysis assigns a circulating current to each window of a planar circuit and writes KVL around each window. It is the systematic version of “write loop equations,” and it usually gives fewer equations than branch-current KCL/KVL.

Definition and intuition

A mesh is a loop that contains no other loop. In a planar circuit drawn on paper, the meshes are the visible windows. Give each window its own current, all circulating clockwise (the choice is arbitrary, but be consistent). The mesh current is a mathematical current, not necessarily a physical branch current.

Why it works: each mesh current automatically satisfies KCL at every node inside the mesh, because whatever flows in along one side flows out along another. Only KVL remains to be imposed. Shared resistors carry the difference of the two adjacent mesh currents, which is where the coupling terms come from.

Rules

  • Use mesh analysis only on a planar circuit (one that can be drawn without crossing wires).
  • Assign a clockwise current to every mesh: I1, I2, ….
  • For mesh 1, write KVL in the direction of I1. A resistor shared with mesh 2 has current (I1 − I2) in the direction of I1.
  • Put voltage rises on one side and I R drops on the other, or set the whole sum to zero. Do not mix conventions halfway through.
  • A current source on an outer branch fixes that mesh current directly. A current source shared by two meshes would need a supermesh; that is excluded, so transform the source first.
Mesh 1: R11I1 − R12I2 = ΣV1   ·   Mesh 2: −R21I1 + R22I2 = ΣV2

R11 is the sum of all resistors in mesh 1; R12 is the resistor shared by meshes 1 and 2 (positive in this form). The right side is the net voltage rise in the direction of that mesh current.

Worked example — two meshes, two sources

Given. Left mesh: 12 V source, 2 Ω top resistor, 4 Ω shared resistor. Right mesh: 4 Ω shared, 2 Ω top resistor, 6 V source (Figure 5). Both mesh currents clockwise.

Required. I1, I2 and the current through the 4 Ω resistor.

KVL, mesh 1 (clockwise). Rise 12 V, drop 2I1, drop 4(I1 − I2):

12 − 2I1 − 4(I1 − I2) = 0  →  3I1 − 2I2 = 6

KVL, mesh 2 (clockwise). Drop 4(I2 − I1), drop 2I2, then the 6 V source is crossed from + to −, which is a drop:

−4(I2 − I1) − 2I2 − 6 = 0  →  2I1 − 3I2 = 3

Solve. From the first equation, I2 = (3I1 − 6)/2. Substitute into the second:

2I1 − 3(3I1 − 6)/2 = 3  →  4I1 − 9I1 + 18 = 6  →  −5I1 = −12
I1 = 2.4 A   ·   I2 = 0.6 A

Shared branch. Downwards through the 4 Ω: I1 − I2 = 1.8 A.

Check. Voltage at the top of the shared resistor: 12 − 2(2.4) = 7.2 V; from the right, 6 + 2(0.6) = 7.2 V. The shared current is 7.2/4 = 1.8 A, which equals 2.4 − 0.6. ✓

Power check. 12 V source delivers 12 × 2.4 = 28.8 W. The 6 V source absorbs 6 × 0.6 = 3.6 W because current enters its + terminal. Resistors absorb 2(2.4²) + 4(1.8²) + 2(0.6²) = 11.52 + 12.96 + 0.72 = 25.2 W. 28.8 − 3.6 = 25.2. ✓

Exam procedure — mesh

  1. Check that the circuit is planar. Redraw it if wires cross unnecessarily.
  2. Label each mesh and draw all mesh currents clockwise.
  3. Write one KVL equation per mesh. For a shared resistor, use the difference of the two mesh currents.
  4. Put the equations in standard form with the mesh currents on the left and source voltages on the right.
  5. Solve. Then convert mesh currents to the branch currents the question actually asks for.
  6. Check one shared-branch voltage from both sides, and check the power balance.

Common mistakes — mesh

  • Writing the shared resistor as R I1 instead of R(I1 − I2).
  • Choosing one mesh clockwise and another anticlockwise, then using a sign rule that assumes both are clockwise.
  • Forgetting that a source can be a drop rather than a rise, depending on the direction of traversal.
  • Putting a current source in the middle of two meshes and inventing a supermesh. That is outside the syllabus; transform the source first.
  • Reporting I1 as the current in every branch of mesh 1. It is only the mesh current.

Nodal analysis

Nodal analysis chooses one node as the reference and writes KCL at every other node. The unknowns are node voltages. It is often faster than mesh when the circuit has many parallel branches or when you need one particular node voltage.

Definition and intuition

Every branch current can be written as (Vnode − Vneighbour)/R. If you sum those expressions at a node and set the sum to zero, you get one equation per unknown node voltage. The reference node is not an unknown; its voltage is 0 V.

The method works because KCL is automatically satisfied once every node equation is satisfied. The equations are linear, so two or three unknowns are solved by substitution or elimination.

Rules

  • Choose the reference node first. The bottom rail is usually best.
  • Label every other node with a voltage V1, V2, … relative to the reference.
  • At each non-reference node, write “sum of currents leaving = 0.” For a resistor R to a neighbour at Vn, the leaving current is (V − Vn)/R.
  • A current source leaving the node contributes +Is to the “leaving” side; entering contributes −Is.
  • A voltage source connected between a node and the reference fixes that node voltage. Do not write KCL there; find the source current last.
  • A voltage source between two non-reference nodes would need a supernode. That is excluded; choose the reference at one end, or transform the source first.
At node 1: (V1 − V2)/R12 + (V1 − V3)/R13 + ⋯ = ΣIsources entering

The left side is the net current leaving through resistors. The right side is the net current injected by sources. Rearranged, this is the same as “sum of leaving currents = 0.”

Worked example — one unknown node

Given. Node A is connected to a 12 V source through 6 Ω, to a 6 V source through 3 Ω, and to ground through 2 Ω (Figure 6).

Required. VA and the current in each branch.

Assumed directions. All branch currents leave A.

KCL at A.

(VA − 12)/6 + (VA − 6)/3 + VA/2 = 0

Substitute and solve. Multiply by 6:

(VA − 12) + 2(VA − 6) + 3VA = 0
6VA = 24  →  VA = 4 V

Branch currents. 6 Ω branch: (4 − 12)/6 = −1.333 A, so 1.333 A flows into A. 3 Ω branch: (4 − 6)/3 = −0.667 A, so 0.667 A flows into A. 2 Ω branch: 4/2 = 2 A leaves A.

Check. 1.333 + 0.667 = 2 A into A; 2 A leaves. ✓

Power check. 12 V source delivers 12 × 1.333 = 16 W. 6 V source delivers 6 × 0.667 = 4 W. Resistors absorb 1.333²×6 + 0.667²×3 + 2²×2 = 10.667 + 1.333 + 8 = 20 W. ✓

Exam procedure — nodal

  1. Mark the reference node (0 V). If a voltage source connects to it, that source fixes the adjacent node voltage.
  2. Label every remaining node with an unknown voltage.
  3. At each unknown node, write KCL with every current expressed as a voltage difference over a resistance.
  4. Multiply through by the least common multiple to clear fractions. Group the voltage terms.
  5. Solve the simultaneous equations. Then find branch currents from the node voltages.
  6. Check KCL at one node and the power balance.

Common mistakes — nodal

  • Using the wrong sign for a source: if the source is 12 V at the far end, the current leaving A is (VA − 12)/R, not (VA + 12)/R.
  • Writing a KCL equation at the reference node as well; it is not independent.
  • Treating a voltage source between two unknown nodes as a normal resistor. That needs a supernode, which is excluded; change the reference or transform the source.
  • Forgetting a branch, especially a current-source branch that does not look like a resistor.
  • Mixing node voltages with branch voltages in the final answer. A branch voltage is a difference of two node voltages.

Star–delta and delta–star conversion

Some networks are neither series nor parallel. A bridge is the standard example: no two resistors are in series, and no two are in parallel, so you cannot reduce it by the basic rules. A star–delta conversion replaces a three-terminal “Y” by an equivalent three-terminal “Δ” (or the reverse), after which the circuit usually collapses into series and parallel combinations.

“…equivalent Δ and Y networks behave identically.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Δ-Y and Y-Δ Conversions”. Chapter link. The work is released for free distribution by the author.

Paraphrase. Two three-terminal networks are equivalent if the resistance measured between any pair of terminals is the same in both, with the third terminal open. Matching the three pairs gives three equations, and solving them gives the conversion formulas. The networks are not physically identical inside; they are identical only as seen from the three terminals.

Delta → star (Δ → Y)

Label the delta resistors RAB, RBC, RCA and the star legs RA, RB, RC (the leg connected to the corresponding terminal). Let S = RAB + RBC + RCA.

RA = RABRCA / S   ·   RB = RABRBC / S   ·   RC = RBCRCA / S

Each star leg is the product of the two delta resistors touching that terminal, divided by the sum of all three.

Star → delta (Y → Δ)

Now let P = RARB + RBRC + RCRA.

RAB = P / RC   ·   RBC = P / RA   ·   RCA = P / RB

Each delta resistor is the sum of the pairwise products, divided by the star leg opposite that delta side.

Balanced shortcut. If all three star legs equal RY, the equivalent delta is RΔ = 3RY. If all three delta resistors equal RΔ, the equivalent star is RY = RΔ/3.

Why the formulas work

Measure the resistance between A and B with C open. In the star, the path is RA + RB. In the delta, the path is RAB in parallel with (RCA + RBC):

RA + RB = RAB(RCA + RBC) / (RAB + RBC + RCA)

Write the corresponding equations for B–C and C–A, then solve the three equations for the star legs. The result is the Δ → Y formula above. Reversing the algebra gives Y → Δ. You do not need to reproduce the derivation unless the question asks for it, but you should know that it comes from terminal equivalence.

Worked example — reduce a bridge with Δ → Y

Given. Terminals A and B. A–C = 4 Ω, A–D = 6 Ω, C–B = 10 Ω, D–B = 30 Ω, C–D = 10 Ω (left panel of Figure 8).

Required. RAB.

Choose the delta. The resistors C–D, D–B and B–C form a delta with terminals C, D, B. Convert this delta to a star with centre S.

Step 1 — sum.

S = 10 + 30 + 10 = 50 Ω

Step 2 — star legs.

RC = (10 × 10)/50 = 2 Ω   ·   RD = (10 × 30)/50 = 6 Ω   ·   RB = (30 × 10)/50 = 6 Ω

Step 3 — redraw. A–C (4 Ω) is in series with C–S (2 Ω), giving 6 Ω. A–D (6 Ω) is in series with D–S (6 Ω), giving 12 Ω. These two branches are in parallel between A and S. S–B is 6 Ω.

Step 4 — series and parallel.

RAS = (6 × 12)/(6 + 12) = 72/18 = 4 Ω
RAB = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω

Check. Inject 1 A from A to B and solve the original bridge by nodal analysis: VA − VB = 10 V, so RAB = 10 Ω. The conversion and the original circuit agree.

Exam procedure — star–delta

  1. Identify a three-terminal star or delta that is not already in series or parallel.
  2. Label its terminals consistently in both the original and the converted drawing. Most errors are label errors, not arithmetic errors.
  3. Choose the direction that makes the remaining circuit simpler. Converting a delta at the “bottom” of a bridge usually works well.
  4. Write the formula with the labels visible. Substitute only after the formula is complete.
  5. Redraw the reduced circuit and finish with series/parallel rules.
  6. Check the equivalent resistance is plausible: it must be smaller than the sum of all resistors and larger than the smallest parallel path.

Common mistakes — star–delta

  • Using the delta formula when the network is a star (or vice versa).
  • Dividing by the wrong sum. For Δ → Y the denominator is the sum of all three delta resistors; for Y → Δ it is the opposite star leg.
  • Mixing the terminal labels after redrawing. Mark A, B, C on both diagrams before substituting.
  • Forgetting that a balanced star of 6 Ω becomes a delta of 18 Ω, not 2 Ω. The factor is 3 in the Y → Δ direction.
  • Applying a conversion to a network that already has a simple series or parallel reduction.

Superposition theorem

Superposition replaces a multi-source problem by several single-source problems. It is the most reliable way to see what each source contributes, and it is often the fastest method when a circuit has one voltage source and one current source.

“The strategy used in the Superposition Theorem is to eliminate all but one source of power within a network at a time…”

“…all other voltage sources are replaced by wires (shorts), and all current sources with open circuits (breaks).”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Superposition Theorem”. Chapter link.

“Superposition Theorem is only applicable for determining voltage and current, not power!!!”

Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Superposition Theorem”. The emphasis is the author’s.

Definition. In a linear circuit with several independent sources, the voltage across (or current through) any element is the algebraic sum of the voltages (or currents) produced by each source acting alone, with every other independent source set to zero.

Why it works. The circuit equations are linear. If x1 solves the equations with source 1 on and the others off, and x2 solves with source 2 on and the others off, then x1 + x2 solves the equations with both on. Linearity is the whole proof.

Worked example — two sources, two contributions

Given. 12 V source through 2 Ω to node A; 4 Ω from A to ground; 6 A source from ground into A (Figure 9).

Required. VA by superposition, and a comment on power.

Contribution 1 — 12 V source alone. Replace the 6 A source by an open circuit. The 2 Ω and 4 Ω are in series across 12 V. By voltage division:

VA′ = 12 × 4/(2 + 4) = 8 V

Contribution 2 — 6 A source alone. Replace the 12 V source by a short circuit. The 2 Ω and 4 Ω are now in parallel from A to ground:

Rp = (2 × 4)/(2 + 4) = 8/6 = 4/3 Ω
VA″ = 6 × 4/3 = 8 V

Add. Both contributions have the same polarity at A (both make A positive), so

VA = 8 + 8 = 16 V

Check with KCL. At 16 V, the current through 2 Ω is (16 − 12)/2 = 2 A, flowing from A toward the 12 V source. The current through 4 Ω is 16/4 = 4 A downward. The 6 A source injects 6 A into A. 2 + 4 = 6. ✓

Power warning. Actual power in the 4 Ω resistor is 16²/4 = 64 W. The separate contributions would give 8²/4 = 16 W and 8²/4 = 16 W, whose sum is only 32 W. Superposition does not apply to power, because power is proportional to the square of voltage or current, not to the first power.

Rules

  • Turn on one independent source at a time. Replace every other voltage source by a short (0 V) and every other current source by an open (0 A).
  • Solve the reduced circuit for the required voltage or current. Keep the assumed direction or polarity fixed for every contribution.
  • Add the contributions algebraically. A contribution opposite to the chosen reference direction enters with a minus sign.
  • Do not use superposition on power. Find the total voltage or current first, then calculate power once.
  • Superposition requires a linear circuit. Resistors, independent sources and linear dependent sources qualify; diodes and other non-linear elements do not.

Exam procedure — superposition

  1. Label the required voltage or current with a definite polarity or direction. Keep that reference fixed.
  2. Redraw the circuit once per source. Mark clearly which source is active.
  3. In each reduced circuit, use series/parallel, voltage division, current division or a single KCL equation.
  4. Record each contribution with its sign relative to the chosen reference.
  5. Add. Then check the final result in the original circuit with KCL or KVL.
  6. If the question asks for power, calculate it from the total voltage or current, not from the sum of contributions.

Common mistakes — superposition

  • Replacing a voltage source by an open circuit or a current source by a short. It is the other way round: voltage → short, current → open.
  • Forgetting to switch off all other sources, not just the obvious one.
  • Adding contributions with inconsistent polarities. Fix the reference before you start.
  • Superposing power. This is the most common conceptual error in the whole module.
  • Using superposition with a dependent source as if it were independent. Dependent sources are outside this syllabus and need a different treatment.

Thevenin’s theorem

Thevenin’s theorem replaces any linear two-terminal network by one voltage source and one series resistance. It is the standard tool for “find the current in this one load” questions, and it is the foundation of the maximum power transfer theorem.

“Thevenin's Theorem states that it is possible to simplify any linear circuit, no matter how complex, to an equivalent circuit with just a single voltage source and series resistance connected to a load.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Thevenin’s Theorem”. Chapter link.

Definition. Look into the two terminals of a linear network with the load removed. The network behaves, from those terminals, exactly like a source Vth in series with a resistance Rth.

  • Vth is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals with the load removed.
  • Rth is the resistance seen looking into the terminals with every independent source killed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened).
  • Equivalently, Rth = Voc / Isc, where Isc is the short-circuit current.
Worked example — Thevenin with a current source

Given. 12 V source in series with 3 Ω to node A; 6 Ω from A to ground; 6 A source from ground into A; load 2 Ω from A to ground (Figure 10a).

Required. Vth, Rth, and the load current.

Step 1 — remove the load. The 2 Ω branch is open. The 12 V source, 3 Ω, 6 Ω and 6 A source remain.

Step 2 — find Vth. Let VA be the open-circuit voltage. KCL at A, with the 6 A source entering:

(VA − 12)/3 + VA/6 = 6

Multiply by 6:

2(VA − 12) + VA = 36  →  3VA = 60  →  Vth = 20 V

Step 3 — find Rth. Kill the sources: short the 12 V source, open the 6 A source. Looking into A–B, the 3 Ω and 6 Ω are in parallel:

Rth = (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) = 18/9 = 2 Ω

Step 4 — reconnect the load.

IL = Vth/(Rth + RL) = 20/(2 + 2) = 5 A
VL = 5 × 2 = 10 V   ·   PL = 5² × 2 = 50 W

Check by solving the original circuit with the load connected. KCL at A:

(V − 12)/3 + V/6 + V/2 = 6
2(V − 12) + V + 3V = 36  →  6V = 60  →  V = 10 V

Then IL = 10/2 = 5 A. ✓

When to use Thevenin

  • The question asks for the current or voltage in one load, and the rest of the network is complicated.
  • The load is changed several times. Find Vth and Rth once, then reconnect each load.
  • You need a two-terminal model for a larger system (a sensor, a source, a sub-circuit).
  • You need Rth for maximum power transfer.

Exam procedure — Thevenin

  1. Identify and remove the load. Mark the two terminals A and B.
  2. Find Vth = VAB with the load removed. Use KCL, KVL, superposition or source transformation.
  3. Kill every independent source: voltage sources become shorts, current sources become opens. Find Rth looking into A–B.
  4. Draw the Thevenin equivalent and reconnect the load.
  5. Calculate the load current or voltage.
  6. Check by solving the original circuit with the load connected, or by the power balance.

Common mistakes — Thevenin

  • Leaving the load in the circuit while finding Rth. Remove it first.
  • Killing a voltage source by opening it, or a current source by shorting it. Reverse that.
  • Finding Vth with the load still connected. That gives the loaded voltage, not the open-circuit voltage.
  • Reversing the polarity of Vth. Mark A and B and keep them fixed.
  • Using Thevenin for power inside the network. The equivalent preserves terminal behaviour, not the internal power distribution.
  • Applying Thevenin to a non-linear network, or to a network with dependent sources without the extra method. Dependent sources are outside this syllabus.

Norton’s theorem

Norton’s theorem is the current-source twin of Thevenin’s theorem. The same linear network becomes a current source in parallel with a resistance.

“Norton's Theorem states that it is possible to simplify any linear circuit, no matter how complex, to an equivalent circuit with just a single current source and parallel resistance connected to a load.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Norton’s Theorem”. Chapter link.

Definition. IN is the short-circuit current that flows from A to B when the load is replaced by a wire. RN is the resistance looking into the terminals with all independent sources killed; it is the same as Rth.

IN = Isc   ·   RN = Rth   ·   Vth = INRN
Worked example — Norton by the short-circuit current

Given. The same network as the Thevenin example: 12 V through 3 Ω to A, 6 Ω from A to ground, 6 A into A; load 2 Ω (Figure 10a).

Required. IN, RN and the load current.

Step 1 — short A to B. With A shorted to ground, the 6 Ω is bypassed and carries no current. The 3 Ω branch has the full 12 V across it, so it carries 12/3 = 4 A from the source to A. The 6 A source also drives current into A. Both currents leave through the short from A to B:

IN = 4 + 6 = 10 A

Step 2 — RN. Kill the sources exactly as for Thevenin: 3 Ω ∥ 6 Ω = 2 Ω.

Step 3 — reconnect the load. The Norton source and RN are in parallel, and the load is also in parallel. Use the current divider:

IL = IN × RN/(RN + RL) = 10 × 2/(2 + 2) = 5 A

Check. Vth = INRN = 10 × 2 = 20 V, which matches the Thevenin calculation. The load current 5 A matches. ✓

Direction convention. IN is defined as the short-circuit current from A to B. In the equivalent circuit, the current source arrow points from B to A (toward the positive terminal A), so that it drives current out of A into the external load. If your arrow is drawn the other way, use a negative value; do not silently change the sign.

When to use Norton

  • The circuit has several parallel branches and a current-source model is more natural.
  • You want to use source transformation or a current divider.
  • The question explicitly says “using Norton’s theorem.”
  • You need a two-terminal current-source model for a sub-circuit.

Exam procedure — Norton

  1. Remove the load and mark A and B.
  2. Short A to B. Find the current through the short, from A to B. That is IN.
  3. Kill all independent sources and find the resistance looking into A–B. That is RN = Rth.
  4. Draw IN in parallel with RN, with the arrow pointing toward A.
  5. Reconnect the load and use a current divider, or convert to Thevenin and use the voltage divider.
  6. Check Vth = INRN against an independent calculation.

Common mistakes — Norton

  • Using the open-circuit voltage as IN. IN is the short-circuit current.
  • Putting RN in series with the current source. It is in parallel.
  • Reversing the arrow and then reporting the load current with the wrong sign.
  • Forgetting that the 6 Ω branch is bypassed by the short, so it contributes no current to IN.
  • Finding RN with the load still connected.

Maximum power transfer theorem

Maximum power transfer answers a specific question: for a fixed source network, what load resistance receives the most power? It is not the same as the load that makes the system most efficient.

“…the maximum amount of power will be dissipated by a load resistance when that load resistance is equal to the Thevenin/Norton resistance of the network supplying the power.”

“Maximum power transfer does not coincide with maximum efficiency.”

Tony R. Kuphaldt, Lessons In Electric Circuits, Volume I — DC, Chapter 10, section “Maximum Power Transfer Theorem”. Chapter link.

Statement. A load receives maximum power from a linear network when RL = Rth (equivalently RN). The maximum power is

Pmax = Vth² / (4Rth) = IN²RN / 4

Both forms are the same statement, because Vth = INRN.

Derivation

Replace the network by its Thevenin equivalent. The load current is

IL = Vth / (Rth + RL)

So the load power is

PL = IL²RL = Vth²RL / (Rth + RL)²

Differentiate with respect to RL and set the derivative to zero:

dPL/dRL = Vth²(Rth − RL) / (Rth + RL)³ = 0

The only positive solution is RL = Rth. Substituting back:

Pmax = Vth²Rth / (2Rth)² = Vth² / (4Rth)
Worked example — maximum power for the Thevenin network

Given. Vth = 20 V, Rth = 2 Ω.

Required. RL for maximum power, Pmax, and the efficiency at that point.

Step 1 — matching condition.

RL = Rth = 2 Ω

Step 2 — maximum power.

Pmax = 20²/(4 × 2) = 400/8 = 50 W

Step 3 — check at the terminals. With RL = 2 Ω, IL = 20/4 = 5 A, VL = 10 V, PL = 5² × 2 = 50 W. ✓

Step 4 — efficiency. The source delivers 20 × 5 = 100 W. The load receives 50 W. Efficiency = 50/100 = 50%. The other 50 W is lost in Rth.

Interpretation. Maximum power is not maximum efficiency. If efficiency matters (power systems), a much larger RL is used; if the goal is to deliver the most signal power (communications, audio), matching is used.

Exam procedure — maximum power

  1. Remove the load and find the Thevenin equivalent of the network facing it.
  2. Set RL = Rth.
  3. Calculate Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth).
  4. If the question asks for the load voltage or current at maximum power, use VL = Vth/2 and IL = Vth/(2Rth).
  5. State the efficiency if it is relevant; it is 50% at maximum power.
  6. Check the units: volts squared divided by ohms gives watts.

Common mistakes — maximum power

  • Setting RL equal to the load in the original circuit instead of Rth.
  • Using Pmax = Vth²/Rth and forgetting the factor 4.
  • Confusing maximum power with maximum efficiency. At maximum power, half the power is lost in the source resistance.
  • Forgetting to remove the load before finding Vth and Rth.
  • Applying the theorem to a network whose source resistance cannot be modelled as a constant (non-linear source).

There is no single “best” method. The circuit shape and the question decide. Use this table as a decision aid, not as a set of laws.

A 15-mark answer template

  1. Read and restate. Write “Given”, “Required”, and the assumed current directions or voltage polarities. Draw a clean labelled diagram.
  2. Choose and justify. One sentence: “Using nodal analysis because there is one unknown node and three parallel branches.”
  3. Write the laws before substituting. A symbolic equation earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips.
  4. Substitute with units. Keep the numbers and units visible. Do not skip the intermediate step that shows the equation is correctly formed.
  5. Solve and state. Give the exact fraction and the decimal, with the unit and the direction.

“Find the value of resistance RL for maximum power transfer and calculate the maximum power.” A 100 V source feeds a bridge: 10 Ω and 20 Ω on the top arms, 30 Ω and 40 Ω on the bottom arms, and RL between the midpoints.

Answer: RL = 125/6 Ω ≈ 20.83 Ω; Pmax = 5/6 W ≈ 0.833 W.

A2-Q4 · Maximum power with a source in the load branch

June 2025 · Q1(a) · Star to delta · 3 marks
University of Mumbai, NEP 2020, BEEE (N10415), 13 June 2025, Q1(a). Compulsory question.

Question as printed. “Convert the star circuit into its equivalent delta circuit.” The star has RA = 2 Ω from A to the star point N, RB = 4 Ω from B to N, and RC = 6 Ω from C to N.

Answer. RAB = 22/3 Ω, RBC = 22 Ω, RCA = 11 Ω.

Working. The sum of the pairwise products is

P = (2 × 4) + (4 × 6) + (6 × 2) = 8 + 24 + 12 = 44 Ω²

Each delta side is P divided by the star leg opposite that side:

RAB = 44/6 = 22/3 Ω  ·  RBC = 44/2 = 22 Ω  ·  RCA = 44/4 = 11 Ω

Check. The resistance between A and B with C open must be the same in both networks. Star: 2 + 4 = 6 Ω. Delta: 22/3 in parallel with (22 + 11) = (22/3 × 33)/(22/3 + 33) = 242/(121/3) = 6 Ω. Good. Do the same check for B–C and C–A if time allows.

June 2025 · Q2(a) · Norton through 3 Ω · 10 marks
University of Mumbai, NEP 2020, BEEE (N10415), 13 June 2025, Q2(a). This is the same circuit as Assignment 1, Q3.

Question as printed. “Using Norton’s theorem, calculate the current through 3 Ω resistor across the terminals A and B in the given circuit.” The network has a 15 V source with 2 Ω on the left, 8 Ω across the top, a 16 Ω diagonal, a 5 V source with 10 Ω in the centre, 2 Ω on the right and 2 Ω along the bottom; the 3 Ω load is between A (top right) and B (bottom right).

Answer. I3Ω = 41/48 A ≈ 0.854 A from A to B, taking the 15 V source positive at the top-left node, the 5 V source positive at its top node, and the 3 Ω load between A and B.

Working. Remove the 3 Ω load and solve the remaining network by nodal analysis. The open-circuit voltage is

Vth = 205/26 V ≈ 7.885 V

With the 15 V and 5 V sources replaced by short circuits, the resistance looking into A–B is

Rth = 81/13 Ω ≈ 6.231 Ω

The Norton current is IN = Vth/Rth = (205/26)/(81/13) = 205/162 A ≈ 1.265 A, and RN = 81/13 Ω. Reconnect the 3 Ω load:

I3Ω = Vth/(Rth + 3) = (205/26)/(120/13) = 41/48 A

June 2025 · Q4(a) · Nodal analysis through 4 Ω · 5 marks
University of Mumbai, NEP 2020, BEEE (N10415), 13 June 2025, Q4(a).

Question as printed. “Find the current through 4 Ω resistance using Nodal analysis.” The circuit has a 6 V source (positive terminal at the top) in series with 3 Ω from the bottom rail to node A; 6 Ω from A to the bottom rail; 4 Ω from A to B; 2 Ω from B to the bottom rail; and a 5 A source from the bottom rail to B. The arrow on the 5 A source points upward in the scanned paper.

Answer. With the 5 A arrow upward, I4Ω = 0.75 A from B to A. If your copy shows the 5 A arrow downward, the answer becomes 1.75 A from A to B. State the arrow direction with the answer.

Working (5 A upward). Take the bottom rail as 0 V. The 6 V source and 3 Ω put the left branch in series, so KCL at A and B gives

(VA − 6)/3 + VA/6 + (VA − VB)/4 = 0
(VB − VA)/4 + VB/2 − 5 = 0

Multiply the first by 12 and the second by 4:

9VA − 3VB = 24  ·  −VA + 3VB = 20
VA = 5.5 V  ·  VB = 8.5 V

The current from A to B is (5.5 − 8.5)/4 = −0.75 A, so the magnitude is 0.75 A and the direction is B to A. Check at B: (8.5 − 5.5)/4 + 8.5/2 − 5 = 0.75 + 4.25 − 5 = 0.

If the 5 A arrow is downward: the second equation becomes (VB − VA)/4 + VB/2 + 5 = 0, giving VA = 0.5 V, VB = −6.5 V, and I4Ω = (0.5 + 6.5)/4 = 1.75 A from A to B. The two readings differ only in the source arrow, which is exactly why the arrow must be copied onto your answer sheet.

June 2025 · Q6(a) · Source transformation through 8 Ω · 6 marks
University of Mumbai, NEP 2020, BEEE (N10415), 13 June 2025, Q6(a).

Question as printed. “Using source transformation find the current flowing the 8 Ω resistance.” The circuit has an 18 V source in series with 6 Ω feeding node C; 6 Ω from C to the bottom rail; 6 Ω from C to node D; a 2 A source from the bottom rail to D (arrow upward); 1 Ω from D to X; and the 8 Ω load from X to Y, with Y on the bottom rail.

Answer. I8Ω = 1.5 A downward (from X to Y).

Working by source transformation.

  1. 18 V in series with 6 Ω becomes 3 A in parallel with 6 Ω; the arrow points from the bottom rail to C.
  2. At C, this 6 Ω is in parallel with the vertical 6 Ω, giving 3 Ω. The 3 A source remains. Transform again: 3 A in parallel with 3 Ω becomes 9 V in series with 3 Ω.
  3. Add the 6 Ω between C and D in series: 9 Ω. The branch from D to the bottom rail is therefore 9 V in series with 9 Ω. Transform it to 1 A in parallel with 9 Ω, arrow from the bottom rail to D.
  4. The 2 A source also points from the bottom rail to D. Add the two parallel current sources: 1 + 2 = 3 A, still in parallel with 9 Ω.
  5. The load path from D to the bottom rail is 1 Ω + 8 Ω = 9 Ω. So the 3 A source feeds 9 Ω in parallel with 9 Ω, equivalent to 4.5 Ω. The voltage at D is 3 × 4.5 = 13.5 V.
  6. The current through the 8 Ω branch is 13.5/(1 + 8) = 1.5 A, from X to Y.

Check by nodal analysis. With the bottom rail at 0 V, the equations at C and D give VC = 10.5 V and VD = 13.5 V; then VX = 12 V and (13.5 − 12)/1 = 1.5 A. Both methods agree.

Sign and unit discipline

  • Mark every source’s + and − before writing an equation. The long line of a battery symbol is positive.
  • Choose mesh currents all clockwise, or all anticlockwise, and keep the choice. A shared branch carries the difference of the two mesh currents.
  • For nodal analysis, mark the reference node with 0 V and write every current as “(node voltage − neighbour voltage)/resistance” for currents leaving the node.
  • A current source fixes the current in its branch, not the voltage across it. A voltage source fixes the voltage between its terminals, not the current through it.
  • Carry units through the arithmetic: Ω × A = V; V × A = W; V²/Ω = W.
  • A negative answer is information, not an error: it means the actual direction is opposite to the assumed arrow. Keep the magnitude and state the true direction.
  • Never add powers from superposition. Superposition applies to voltages and currents in linear circuits, not to power.

Ten-minute checklist before the paper

  1. Can you state KCL and KVL and write both with correct signs?
  2. Can you draw the four source symbols and explain ideal versus practical behaviour?
  3. Can you convert a source in both directions and place the arrow correctly?
  4. Can you write a mesh equation with a shared branch without a sign error?
  5. Can you write a nodal equation at a node with a voltage source behind a resistor?
  6. Can you state both star–delta formulas and the balanced shortcuts?
  7. Can you list the three steps of superposition and the power warning?
  8. Can you find Vth, Rth, IN and RN for a two-source network?
  9. Can you derive RL = Rth and Pmax?
  10. Can you check a final answer by substituting it back into one KCL or KVL equation?
