1The big idea

A square matrix \(A\) acts on a column vector \(X\) and gives back a new vector \(AX\). Most vectors change direction. A few special ones keep their direction and are only stretched or shrunk. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue.

\[ AX = \lambda X, \qquad X \neq 0 \]

\(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix, \(X\) is a non-zero column vector (the eigenvector) and \(\lambda\) is a number (the eigenvalue). The zero vector is excluded because \(A\cdot0=\lambda\cdot0\) holds for every \(\lambda\) and tells you nothing.

Definitions from the notes

NameMeaning
Characteristic matrix\(A-\lambda I\), where \(I\) is the unit matrix of the same order
Characteristic equation\(|A-\lambda I|=0\)
EigenvaluesThe roots of that equation (also called characteristic, latent or proper values)
Eigenvector for \(\lambda\)A non-zero column \(X\) with \([A-\lambda I]X=0\)

We solve \(|A-\lambda I|=0\) because \([A-\lambda I]X=0\) has a non-zero solution only when \(A-\lambda I\) is singular. For a \(3\times3\) matrix you also get two quick checks:

\[ \lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=\text{sum of the diagonal elements},\qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3=|A| \]

2The recipe, with a full example

  1. Write \(|A-\lambda I|=0\).
  2. Solve it to get the roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3\).
  3. For each root, form \([A-\lambda_1 I]x=0\).
  4. Row-reduce that matrix to echelon form.
  5. With \(n\) unknowns and rank \(r\), you get \(n-r\) independent parameters.
  6. Use Cramer's rule when it is quicker.
  7. Solve and write the eigenvectors.
Shortcut for a 3×3 characteristic equation \[ \lambda^3 - S_1\lambda^2 + S_2\lambda - |A| = 0 \]

\(S_1\) is the sum of the diagonal elements. \(S_2\) is the sum of the three \(2\times2\) minors found by deleting row \(i\) and column \(i\) in turn. Every example in the notes starts this way.

Cramer's cross-multiplication. For two equations \(a_1x+b_1y+c_1z=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2z=0\), take two rows of \(A-\lambda I\) that are not multiples of each other:

\[ \frac{x}{\begin{vmatrix}b_1&c_1\\b_2&c_2\end{vmatrix}}=\frac{-y}{\begin{vmatrix}a_1&c_1\\a_2&c_2\end{vmatrix}}=\frac{z}{\begin{vmatrix}a_1&b_1\\a_2&b_2\end{vmatrix}} \]
Worked example notes pp. 2–4

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&1\\1&2&-1\\1&-1&2\end{bmatrix} \]

Characteristic equation. \(S_1=6\), \(S_2=(4-1)+(4-1)+(4+1)=11\), \(|A|=6\), so

\[ \lambda^3-6\lambda^2+11\lambda-6=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda=1,2,3 \]

\(\lambda=1\). \(A-I\) gives \(x_1-x_2+x_3=0\) and \(x_1+x_2-x_3=0\). Adding them gives \(x_1=0\), then \(x_2=x_3\).

\[ X_1=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\lambda=2\). \(A-2I\) gives \(-x_2+x_3=0\) and \(x_1-x_3=0\), so all entries are equal.

\[ X_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\lambda=3\). \(A-3I\) gives \(-x_1-x_2+x_3=0\) and \(x_1-x_2-x_3=0\). Adding them gives \(x_2=0\), then \(x_1=x_3\).

\[ X_3=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

Check: \(AX_3=[3,0,3]^T=3X_3\).

Independent? Put \(k_1X_1+k_2X_2+k_3X_3=0\). This gives \(k_2+k_3=0\), \(k_1+k_2=0\) and \(k_1+k_2+k_3=0\). The last minus the second gives \(k_3=0\), then \(k_2=0\) and \(k_1=0\). Only the zero combination works, so the vectors are linearly independent.

Watch out. Any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, so \([0,2,2]^T\) and \([0,1,1]^T\) are both right. The notes reduce to the smallest whole numbers, so do the same in the exam.

3The four types of problem

Your teacher sorts every question by two yes/no tests: is the matrix symmetric (\(A=A^T\)), and are the eigenvalues distinct or repeated? The method never changes. What changes is how many eigenvectors a repeated root gives.

Type II notes pp. 5–6 \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}8&-6&2\\-6&7&-4\\2&-4&3\end{bmatrix},\quad S_1=18,\; S_2=45,\; |A|=0 \] \[ \lambda^3-18\lambda^2+45\lambda=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=0,3,15 \]

Using the first two rows with Cramer's rule for each root:

\(\lambda\)Cross-productsEigenvector
0\(\tfrac{x_1}{10}=\tfrac{x_2}{20}=\tfrac{x_3}{20}\)\([1,\,2,\,2]^T\)
3\(\tfrac{x_1}{16}=\tfrac{x_2}{8}=\tfrac{x_3}{-16}\)\([2,\,1,\,-2]^T\)
15\(\tfrac{x_1}{40}=\tfrac{x_2}{-40}=\tfrac{x_3}{20}\)\([2,\,-2,\,1]^T\)

Check: \(0+3+15=18=S_1\) and \(0\cdot3\cdot15=0=|A|\). Extra, not in the notes: for a symmetric matrix the eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular, e.g. \([1,2,2]\cdot[2,1,-2]=0\).

Type III notes pp. 7–8 \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&2&1\\1&3&1\\1&2&2\end{bmatrix},\quad \lambda^3-7\lambda^2+11\lambda-5=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=1,1,5 \]

\(\lambda=1\) (repeated). \(A-I\) has three identical rows \([1,2,1]\), so the rank is \(r=1\) and there are \(n-r=2\) independent parameters. The one equation is \(x_1+2x_2+x_3=0\). Put \(x_2=s,\,x_3=t\), so \(x_1=-2s-t\). Two eigenvectors:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{and}\ \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\lambda=5\). Row reduction gives \(x_1+2x_2-3x_3=0\) and \(-4x_2+4x_3=0\), so \(x_1=x_2=x_3\). Rank 2, one parameter:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]
Type IV notes pp. 8–9 \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1&1\\-1&3&-1\\1&-1&3\end{bmatrix},\quad \lambda^3-9\lambda^2+24\lambda-20=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=2,2,5 \]

\(\lambda=2\). \(A-2I\) reduces to \(x_1-x_2+x_3=0\). Rank 1, so 2 parameters. With \(x_2=s,\,x_3=t\) we get \(x_1=s-t\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{and}\ \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\lambda=5\). The first two rows give \(\tfrac{x_1}{3}=\tfrac{x_2}{-3}=\tfrac{x_3}{3}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

The rule that decides everything. A root gives \(n-r\) independent eigenvectors, where \(r\) is the rank of \(A-\lambda I\). A repeated root may give fewer vectors than its repeat count. Chapter 6 uses this to decide whether a matrix can be diagonalised.

4Properties of eigenvalues

The syllabus asks for these without proof. Knowing them lets you skip the full calculation in many questions.

  • Theorem 1. \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) exactly when some non-zero \(X\) satisfies \(AX=\lambda X\).
  • Theorem 2. If \(X\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda\), so is \(kX\) for any non-zero scalar \(k\).
  • Theorem 3. One eigenvector cannot belong to two different eigenvalues.
  • Theorem 4. Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

5Cayley–Hamilton theorem

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. If \(\lambda^3-S_1\lambda^2+S_2\lambda-|A|=0\), then

\[ A^3-S_1A^2+S_2A-|A|\,I=0 \]

The constant term becomes \(|A|\) times the identity matrix, not a bare number. No proof is needed for the exam.

You will be asked to verify it, to find \(A^{-1}\), or to reduce a high power of \(A\).

Verify, then find \(A^{-1}\) and \(A^4\) notes pp. 14–15 \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&-1&4\\3&1&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad S_1=-1,\; S_2=-18,\; |A|=40 \] \[ \lambda^3+\lambda^2-18\lambda-40=0 \;\Rightarrow\; A^3+A^2-18A-40I=0 \tag{1} \]

Verify. The powers are

\[ A^2=\begin{bmatrix}14&3&8\\12&9&-2\\2&4&14\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^3=\begin{bmatrix}44&33&46\\24&13&74\\52&14&8\end{bmatrix} \]

and \(A^3+A^2-18A-40I\) is the zero matrix entry by entry. Top-left: \(44+14-18-40=0\). Middle: \(13+9+18-40=0\).

Inverse. Multiply (1) by \(A^{-1}\): \(A^2+A-18I-40A^{-1}=0\), so

\[ A^{-1}=\frac1{40}\left(A^2+A-18I\right)=\frac1{40}\begin{bmatrix}-3&5&11\\14&-10&2\\5&5&-5\end{bmatrix} \]

Fourth power. Multiply (1) by \(A\): \(A^4=40A+18A^2-A^3\), which gives

\[ A^4=\begin{bmatrix}248&101&218\\272&109&50\\104&98&204\end{bmatrix} \]

The notes also verify the theorem for \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2&1\\1&2&2\\3&2&1\end{bmatrix}\), where \(\lambda^3-6\lambda^2+2\lambda=0\), using \(A^2=\begin{bmatrix}14&12&8\\11&10&7\\14&12&8\end{bmatrix}\) and \(A^3=\begin{bmatrix}78&68&46\\64&56&38\\78&68&46\end{bmatrix}\). Try one entry: top-left is \(78-84+6=0\).

Reducing a long polynomial. Divide it by the characteristic polynomial. The quotient part becomes zero at \(A\), so only the remainder is left. Practice question 4 is built this way.

Watch out. \(A^{-1}\) exists only if \(|A|\neq0\). If the constant term of the characteristic equation is 0, the inverse method fails.

6Similarity and diagonalisation

\(B\) is similar to \(A\) if there is a non-singular matrix \(M\) with \(B=M^{-1}AM\).

Diagonalisation theorem. If \(A\) has distinct eigenvalues, then \(D=M^{-1}AM\) is diagonal. \(M\) has the eigenvectors as its columns, and \(D\) has the eigenvalues on its diagonal in the same order.

For a repeated eigenvalue there are two counts. The algebraic multiplicity is how many times it is repeated as a root. The geometric multiplicity is the number of independent eigenvectors it has, which is \(n-r\).

SituationDiagonalisable?
All eigenvalues distinctYes, always
Repeated roots, and geometric = algebraic for every rootYes
A repeated root with geometric < algebraicNo
Example 1: distinct roots notes pp. 17–18

The Type II matrix from Chapter 3 has eigenvalues \(0,3,15\), so it is diagonalisable. List the eigenvectors in the same order:

\[ M=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&-2\\2&-2&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\0&3&0\\0&0&15\end{bmatrix} \]

and \(M^{-1}AM=D\).

Example 2: a repeated root that still works notes pp. 19–20 \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}-9&4&4\\-8&3&4\\-16&8&7\end{bmatrix},\quad \lambda^3-\lambda^2-5\lambda-3=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=-1,-1,3 \]

\(\lambda=-1\). \(A+I\) has rows that are all multiples of \([-2,1,1]\), so \(r=1\) and \(n-r=2\). Algebraic 2 equals geometric 2. Eigenvectors \([1,2,0]^T\) and \([1,0,2]^T\).

\(\lambda=3\). \(r=2\), so one eigenvector, \([1,1,2]^T\). Algebraic 1 equals geometric 1.

Both match, so \(A\) is diagonalisable:

\[ D=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\2&0&1\\0&2&2\end{bmatrix} \]
Example 3: not diagonalisable extra, not in the notes

The syllabus names non-diagonalisable matrices, but the teacher's notes only show diagonalisable cases. The smallest useful one:

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lambda=2,2,3 \]

For \(\lambda=2\), \(A-2I=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has rank 2, so there is only \(3-2=1\) eigenvector. Algebraic 2 is bigger than geometric 1, so \(A\) is not diagonalisable.

Wording in your notes. On page 16, notes (iii) and (v) say "algebraic" where "geometric" is meant. The number of independent eigenvectors is the geometric multiplicity. Use the definitions above in the exam.

Self-learning topics. The syllabus lists these for Module 1 separately: derogatory and non-derogatory matrices, functions of a square matrix, linear transformations, quadratic forms. The teacher's notes do not cover them, so they are not taught here.

Quick recall

Say the answer aloud, then open the line.

What is the defining equation of an eigenvalue and eigenvector?
\(AX=\lambda X\) with \(X\neq0\), equivalent to \([A-\lambda I]X=0\).
Why do we solve \(|A-\lambda I|=0\)?
A non-zero \(X\) exists only when \(A-\lambda I\) is singular, so its determinant is zero.
Sum and product of the eigenvalues?
The sum of the diagonal elements, and \(|A|\).
How do you build the 3×3 characteristic equation quickly?
\(\lambda^3-S_1\lambda^2+S_2\lambda-|A|=0\), with \(S_1\) the diagonal sum and \(S_2\) the sum of the three diagonal minors.
How many eigenvectors does a root give?
\(n-r\), where \(r\) is the rank of \(A-\lambda I\).
Eigenvalues of \(A^{-1}\), \(A^m\), \(kA\) and \(\operatorname{adj}A\)?
\(1/\lambda\), \(\lambda^m\), \(k\lambda\) and \(|A|/\lambda\).
Eigenvalues of a triangular matrix?
Its diagonal entries.
State Cayley–Hamilton and give two uses.
Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. Use it to find \(A^{-1}\) and to reduce higher powers of \(A\).
When is \(A\) diagonalisable?
When geometric equals algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee it.
In \(D=M^{-1}AM\), what are \(M\) and \(D\)?
\(M\) has the eigenvectors as columns. \(D\) is diagonal with the eigenvalues in the matching order.

Q1

(a) Triangular, so the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: \(3,-2,6\).

(b) Sum \(=7\), product \(=3\cdot(-2)\cdot6=-36\).

(c) \(A^2\): \(9,4,36\). \(A^{-1}\): \(\tfrac13,-\tfrac12,\tfrac16\). \(2A\): \(6,-4,12\).

Q2

\(\lambda^3-\lambda^2-5\lambda-3=0\), so \(\lambda=-1,-1,3\). This is the matrix from Chapter 6, Example 2.

For \(\lambda=-1\): \([1,2,0]^T\) and \([1,0,2]^T\). For \(\lambda=3\): \([1,1,2]^T\).

Q3

The eigenvalues are \(1,2,3\). All three are distinct, so by the diagonalisation theorem \(A\) is diagonalisable.

Q4

\(\lambda^3-7\lambda^2+16\lambda-12=0\), so \(A^3-7A^2+16A-12I=0\). Verify by computing \(A^2\) and \(A^3\).

Divide \(\lambda^6-6\lambda^5+9\lambda^4+4\lambda^3-12\lambda^2+2\lambda-1\) by \(\lambda^3-7\lambda^2+16\lambda-12\). The quotient is \(\lambda^3+\lambda^2\) and the remainder is \(2\lambda-1\). The characteristic polynomial vanishes at \(A\), so the expression equals

\[ 2A-I=\begin{bmatrix}5&20&10\\-4&-7&-8\\6&10&13\end{bmatrix} \]
Q5

Expanding along the first column, \(|A-\lambda I|=(1-\lambda)(\lambda^2-\lambda-24)\). The eigenvalues are \(1\) and \(\tfrac{1\pm\sqrt{97}}2\), three distinct real numbers, so \(A\) is diagonalisable.

\(D\) is diagonal with these three values. \(M\) has the matching eigenvectors as columns. For \(\lambda=1\) the eigenvector is \([1,0,0]^T\). The other two involve \(\sqrt{97}\).

Q6

\(S_1=5\), \(S_2=9\), \(|A|=1\), so \(A^3-5A^2+9A-I=0\). Multiply by \(A^{-1}\): \(A^2-5A+9I-A^{-1}=0\), hence \(A^{-1}=A^2-5A+9I\).

Q7

\(\lambda^2-5\lambda-2=0\). \(A^2=\begin{bmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{bmatrix}\) and \(A^2-5A=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}=2I\), so \(A^2-5A-2I=0\).

Multiply by \(A^{-1}\): \(A-5I-2A^{-1}=0\), so \(A^{-1}=\tfrac12(A-5I)=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\\tfrac32&-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}\).

Q8

The eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(1,2,3\) and \(|A|=6\). For \(\operatorname{adj}A\): \(6,3,2\). For \(A^3\): \(1,8,27\).

Q9

\(\lambda=1\) is repeated, so its algebraic multiplicity is 2. \(A-I=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\) has rank 1, so there is \(2-1=1\) eigenvector. Geometric 1 is less than algebraic 2, so \(A\) is not diagonalisable.

Revision sheet

Core

  • \(AX=\lambda X\), \(X\neq0\). Solve \(|A-\lambda I|=0\), then \([A-\lambda I]X=0\).
  • Sum of eigenvalues is the diagonal sum. Product is \(|A|\).
  • 3×3: \(\lambda^3-S_1\lambda^2+S_2\lambda-|A|=0\).
  • Independent eigenvectors for a root: \(n-r\).

Properties

  • \(A^m\to\lambda^m\), \(A^{-1}\to1/\lambda\), \(kA\to k\lambda\), \(\operatorname{adj}A\to|A|/\lambda\), \(f(A)\to f(\lambda)\).
  • Triangular: the diagonal entries. \(|A|=0\): some eigenvalue is 0.

Cayley–Hamilton

  • Replace \(\lambda\) by \(A\), and the constant term by \(|A|I\).
  • Multiply by \(A^{-1}\) for the inverse, by \(A\) for the next power.

Diagonalisation

  • \(D=M^{-1}AM\). Columns of \(M\) are eigenvectors, \(D\) holds eigenvalues in the same order.
  • Distinct eigenvalues: yes. Repeated: yes only if geometric equals algebraic for every root.
