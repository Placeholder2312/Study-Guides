2. Concept-wise learning

2.1 Why this topic exists

Take a matrix A and multiply a vector v by it: Av. Usually the output points in a completely new direction.

But a few special vectors come out parallel to themselves:

Av=λ v .

The matrix only stretches them (or flips them). Those directions are the matrix's eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are its eigenvalues.

Why engineers care:

  • If you know the eigen-directions, then repeated multiplication An v becomes trivial: each eigen-component is simply multiplied by λn. That is how population models, Markov chains and stability analyses are computed.
  • A symmetric matrix can be completely described by its eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. This is the mathematics behind principal component analysis, vibration modes, and quadratic forms.
  • Diagonalisation is the change of coordinates in which a complicated matrix becomes a diagonal one — the simplest possible matrix.

Your syllabus asks for three things: compute eigen-data; use Cayley–Hamilton to tame high powers of A; and decide when a matrix can be diagonalised.

Source for this section. Interactive Linear Algebra (Margalit & Rabinoff), §5.1, motivates eigenvectors exactly this way and gives the definition used below. Your teacher's notes (page 1) give the same definition in matrix language.

2.2 Prerequisites, in plain language

You need five mechanical skills. If any feels shaky, revise it first — every later method is built from these.

(a) Determinant of a 3×3 matrix

For

A=a11a12a13a21a22a23a31a32a33,

expand along the first row:

| A|=a11a22a23a32a33 −a12a21a23a31a33 +a13a21a22a31a32.

Each 2×2 determinant is ad−bc. The signs alternate +,−,+.

Your teacher also uses the diagonal rule (Sarrus): add the three "down-right" products and subtract the three "down-left" products. Both give the same number.

(b) Matrix multiplication

The entry in row i, column j of AB is (row i of A) · (column j of B). For 3×3 matrices this is nine dot products of three terms each.

(c) The identity matrix

I=100010001,   AI=IA=A.

You will constantly meet A−λ I: subtract λ from each diagonal entry only, leaving off-diagonal entries untouched.

(d) Solving a homogeneous system

[A−λ I]x=0 always has the trivial solution x=0. Eigenvectors are the nonzero solutions, so the system must be singular: that is exactly why we first solve |A−λ I|=0.

If the coefficient matrix has rank r (number of nonzero rows after row reduction), then the number of free parameters is

n−r .

For a 3×3 matrix: rank 2 gives a line of eigenvectors (one parameter), rank 1 gives a plane of eigenvectors (two parameters), rank 3 gives only x=0 (so λ was not an eigenvalue).

(e) Rank, and the "number of independent eigenvectors"

Rank is the number of independent equations, equivalently the number of nonzero rows in row-echelon form. Your teacher writes n−r beside almost every eigenvector calculation; that number is the geometric multiplicity of that eigenvalue (see §2.5).

Where to read this. Interactive Linear Algebra §2.4–2.7 (row reduction, rank) and §5.1; or Nicholson, Linear Algebra with Applications, §3.3, which is written for a first course and uses the same Av=λ v definition.

2.3 Characteristic equation, eigenvalues, eigenvectors

2.3.1 Definitions, with every symbol explained

Let A be an n× n matrix.

TermDefinitionWhat each symbol means
Characteristic matrixA−λ IA is your matrix; λ (lambda) is an unknown number; I is the n× n identity
Characteristic equation| A−λ I|=0The determinant set equal to zero
Characteristic polynomialp(λ)=| A−λ I|A polynomial in λ of degree n
EigenvalueA number λ with | A−λ I|=0Also called characteristic root, latent root, proper value
EigenvectorA nonzero vector x with (A−λ I)x=0, i.e. Ax=λ xAlso called latent vector; x=0 is never allowed

Why "nonzero" matters. If x=0 were allowed, every number λ would satisfy Ax=λ x, and eigenvalues would be meaningless. The definition of an eigenvector therefore excludes the zero vector explicitly. This is stated in ILA §5.1 and in Nicholson §3.3.

2.3.2 Two equivalent forms, and a sign trap

|A−λ I|=0   and   |λ I−A|=0

have the same roots, because λ I−A=−(A−λ I) and for 3×3 matrices the determinant changes sign twice: |−M|=(−1)3|M|=−|M|, applied to the two forms gives the same zero set.

But the polynomials differ by a factor (−1)n:

  • Your teacher and ILA use p(λ)=|A−λ I|. For 3×3 matrices,
|A−λ I|=−λ3+S1λ2−S2λ+| A|,

so the equation |A−λ I|=0 is multiplied by −1 in the notes to give the tidy form λ3−S1λ2+S2λ−|A|=0. Both have the same roots.

  • Nicholson uses cA(x)=det(xI−A), which is monic (leading coefficient +1). For 3×3, cA(x)=λ3−S1λ2+S2λ−|A| — the teacher's form after multiplying by −1.

The two conventions therefore describe the same equation for odd n, and negatives of each other for even n (for 2×2, |A−λ I|=λ2−S1λ+|A| is already monic). The roots are identical in every case, and Cayley–Hamilton is true in either convention (multiply the identity by −1 if needed). So you may use either, but do not mix them inside one calculation. This guide uses your teacher's form, |A−λ I|=0, written as λ3−S1λ2+S2λ−|A|=0.

2.3.3 The 3×3 shortcut your teacher uses

Instead of expanding a 3×3 determinant full of λ's, the notes write

λ3−S1λ2+S2λ−|A|=0

where

  • S1=a11+a22+a33 — the trace (sum of the diagonal entries),
  • S2 — the sum of the **three principal 2×2 minors**:
S2=a22a23a32a33 +a11a13a31a33 +a11a12a21a22,

i.e. delete row 1 and column 1; then row 2 and column 2; then row 3 and column 3.

  • |A| — the determinant.

Why it works. Expanding |A−λ I| produces λ3 from the product of the three diagonal terms (a11−λ)(a22−λ)(a33−λ). The λ2 coefficient collects exactly −S1; the λ coefficient collects exactly S2; the constant term is |A| (put λ=0). This is standard determinant theory, and it is what "properties without proof" refers to — you may quote it.

Immediate checks (use them every time):

λ1+λ2+λ3=S1=trA,   λ1λ2λ3=|A|.

If your three roots do not add to the trace, you have made an arithmetic error.

2.3.4 Recipe: eigenvalues and eigenvectors

This is your teacher's procedure (notes, page 2), with the reason for each step added.

  1. Write |A−λ I|=0. Why: eigenvectors must be nonzero, so the system must be singular.
  2. Expand using the S1,S2,|A| form (for 3×3) and solve for λ1,λ2,λ3. Check: sum = trace, product = determinant.
  3. Take one eigenvalue λ=λ1 and form the matrix A−λ1 I. *This is a numerical matrix now — no λ left.*
  4. Row-reduce to echelon form. Why: to count the rank r and read off the equations.
  5. Count parameters: with n unknowns and rank r, there are n−r independent parameters. This is the geometric multiplicity.
  6. Solve the resulting equations. Many students use Cramer's rule here; your teacher does (see the note below).
  7. Write the eigenvector(s) as column vectors, and check by computing Ax and comparing with λ x.

A note on "Cramer's rule" as used in your notes. In the notes, for a rank-2 matrix, the eigenvector is written as ratios of 2×2 determinants: x1a22a23a32a33 =−x2a21a23a31a33 =x3a21a22a31a32 . These three determinants are the components of the cross product of row 1 and row 2 of the coefficient matrix. A cross product is perpendicular to both rows, hence is automatically a solution of "row · x=0". That is why the method works.

Strictly speaking, Cramer's rule applies to a square system with nonzero determinant and gives a unique solution. Here the determinant is zero, so the name is loose. The construction is the cofactor/cross-product method. It gives correct eigenvectors; just do not try to "apply Cramer's rule" to a 3×3 system that has a whole plane of solutions — use parameters there.

2.3.6 What changes when λ=0

If |A|=0, then λ=0 is an eigenvalue. The equation for its eigenvectors is simply

Ax=0,

i.e. the null space of A itself. Your teacher's second example (notes, pages 5–6) is of this type: the matrix has determinant 0 and eigenvalues 0,3,15. Do not write A−0I and then "divide by λ" anywhere — nothing is being divided.

2.4 Properties of eigenvalues

Your teacher lists four theorems and nine notes (notes, page 10). The syllabus says "properties (without proof)", so you may quote them — but you must be able to use them instantly. Each is given below with a one-line reason, because a remembered reason is harder to misapply.

2.4.1 The four theorems

Theorem 1. λ is an eigenvalue of A iff there is a nonzero vector X with AX=λ X.

This is just the definition restated. It is the version you use in checks.

Theorem 2. If X is an eigenvector for λ, then kX is also an eigenvector for the same λ, for any k≠0.

Reason: A(kX)=k(AX)=k(λ X)=λ(kX). Eigenvectors are directions, not lengths — so [1,1,1]T, [2,2,2]T and [−5,−5,−5]T are the same eigenvector. Never mark a different scaling wrong, and never present X=0.

Theorem 3 (uniqueness). An eigenvector cannot belong to two different eigenvalues.

Reason: if AX=λ X and AX=μ X with X≠0, then (λ−μ)X=0, so λ=μ.

Theorem 4. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

Reason (sketch): a dependence relation among eigenvectors can be pushed through A repeatedly, producing a triangular system that forces every coefficient to zero. This is why "distinct eigenvalues" is the easy route to diagonalisability (§2.7).

If rank A=n−1, adjA has rank 1, so n−1 of its eigenvalues are 0. Not examinable as prescribed content, but do not state a false general formula.

2.4.4 Worked example — eigenvalues of a matrix polynomial (T6)

Question (notes, page 11). Find the eigenvalues of A2−2A+I, where

A=20−201−4004.

Solution. A is upper triangular, so by property (ix) its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries:

λ=2, 1, 4 .

Let f(x)=x2−2x+1=(x−1)2. If AX=λ X, then

f(A)X=(A2−2A+I)X=(λ2−2λ+1)X=f(λ)X,

2.5 Repeated eigenvalues: algebraic vs geometric multiplicity

Everything in §2.3 works whether eigenvalues repeat or not. What changes is how many eigenvectors you get, and that decides diagonalisability.

2.5.1 The two multiplicities

Let λ0 be an eigenvalue of an n× n matrix A.

QuantityMeaningHow to compute
Algebraic multiplicity aHow many times (λ−λ0) divides the characteristic polynomialRead the exponent in the factorised characteristic polynomial
Geometric multiplicity gHow many independent eigenvectors belong to λ0g=n−r, where r=rank(A−λ0I); equivalently dimker(A−λ0I)

For every eigenvalue,

1≤ g≤ a

The lower bound holds because an eigenvalue has at least one eigenvector by definition; the upper bound is a standard theorem (ILA §5.4 states the inequality and uses it as the diagonalisability test).

2.5.2 The diagonalisability criterion

A is diagonalisable⟺ g=a for every eigenvalue of A

Equivalently: A is diagonalisable iff the total number of independent eigenvectors is n. Equivalently: iff there is a basis of ℝn consisting of eigenvectors.

Consequences you must be able to state:

  • **Distinct eigenvalues ⇒ diagonalisable.** Each g=a=1, so the total is n. This is sufficient, not necessary.
  • **Repeated eigenvalues ⇒ maybe.** It depends on whether enough independent eigenvectors exist.
  • A diagonalisable matrix may be singular. Invertibility is irrelevant here.

2.6 Cayley–Hamilton theorem

2.6.1 Statement

Cayley–Hamilton theorem. Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation.

In symbols, if

λn+cn−1λn−1+⋯+c1λ+c0=0

is the characteristic equation of A, then

An+cn−1An−1+⋯+c1A+c0I=0 .

For a 3×3 matrix with your teacher's form:

A3−S1A2+S2A−|A|I=0

The one thing students get wrong: the constant term must be multiplied by I. Writing "−|A|=0" instead of "−|A|I=0" makes the expression a number minus a matrix, which is meaningless.

Why the theorem is useful. It gives a universal relation among I,A,A2,…,An. That lets you (i) verify a characteristic polynomial by substitution, (ii) express A−1 as a polynomial in A without computing cofactors, and (iii) reduce A6, A10, A100 to a combination of I,A,A2. Your syllabus names exactly these three uses: "verification and reduction of higher degree polynomials".

2.6.2 Recipe: verification (4 marks in both papers)

  1. Write |A−λ I|=0 and expand to λ3−S1λ2+S2λ−|A|=0.
  2. Compute A2=A· A.
  3. Compute A3=A2· A.
  4. Substitute into A3−S1A2+S2A−|A|I and add the four matrices.
  5. Show the zero matrix and write "Hence Cayley–Hamilton theorem is verified."

Marking habit: the characteristic polynomial, A2, A3 and the final sum each carry marks. Do not skip the explicit matrices.

2.6.6 Cayley–Hamilton and A−1: the general formula

For a 2×2 matrix with characteristic equation λ2−S1λ+|A|=0,

A2−S1A+|A|I=0  ⟹  A−1=1|A|(S1I−A).

A−1=1|A|(A2−S1A+S2I).

Both require |A|≠0. Check the signs carefully: the S2 term is positive here, because it moves to the other side when you solve for A−1.

2.7 Similarity and diagonalisation

2.7.1 Similar matrices

Definition. Square matrices A and B of the same order are similar if there is a nonsingular matrix M such that B=M−1AM .

Think of M as a change of coordinates: M−1AM is "the same linear map, written in a different basis". Similar matrices therefore share everything intrinsic:

  • the same characteristic polynomial, hence the same eigenvalues (with the same algebraic multiplicities),
  • the same trace, determinant and rank,
  • the same geometric multiplicities, hence the same diagonalisability status.

They generally do not have the same eigenvectors (the eigenvectors are re-expressed in the new basis).

2.7.2 Diagonalisable matrices

Definition. A is diagonalisable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix: D=M−1AM,   D=diag(λ1,…,λn), with M nonsingular.

Diagonalization theorem. An n× n matrix is diagonalisable iff it has n linearly independent eigenvectors.

When that holds, take those eigenvectors as the columns of M:

M=[ X1  X2  ⋯  Xn ],    D=λ10⋯00λ2⋯0⋮⋱⋮00⋯λn.

The order rule (the most common lost mark): column j of M must be an eigenvector for the eigenvalue in position (j,j) of D. If you permute the columns of M, you must permute the diagonal of D in exactly the same way.

2.7.3 Recipe: diagonalise a matrix

  1. Find all eigenvalues and their algebraic multiplicities.
  2. For each eigenvalue, find a basis of its eigenspace (row-reduce A−λ I, use n−r parameters).
  3. Count: if the total number of basis eigenvectors is less than n, stop — the matrix is not diagonalisable. (In an exam, say so explicitly and show the shortfall.)
  4. Otherwise form M with those eigenvectors as columns, and D with the matching eigenvalues on the diagonal.
  5. Check cheaply: AM=MD. (This avoids computing M−1.) If you have time, also state det M≠0.

**Why AM=MD is the right check.** M−1AM=D⟺ AM=MD (multiply on the left by M). Column by column, AM=MD says AXj=λjXj, which is exactly the eigenvector condition. So the check is not just arithmetic — it verifies the whole construction.

4.1 Derogatory and non-derogatory matrices

Enrichment — beyond the prescribed 5 hours.

The minimal polynomial

For a square matrix A, the minimal polynomial m(λ) is the monic polynomial of smallest degree with

m(A)=0 .

Cayley–Hamilton guarantees that the characteristic polynomial c(λ) satisfies c(A)=0, so a minimal polynomial always exists and divides c(λ). It is unique.

The definition

A is derogatory if deg m<deg c=n. A is non-derogatory if deg m=deg c=n.

In words: a derogatory matrix is "annihilated by a polynomial smaller than its characteristic polynomial". Its eigenvalues alone do not pin down its behaviour.

T3 has two distinct eigenvalues but n=3, hence derogatory.   1. A is diagonalisable ⟺ its minimal polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors (no repeated roots).
  2. A is non-derogatory ⟺ every eigenvalue has exactly one Jordan block — equivalently, the geometric multiplicity of every eigenvalue is 1.

4.2 Functions of a square matrix

Enrichment — beyond the prescribed 5 hours.

Polynomials first

For a polynomial p(x)=a0+a1x+⋯+akxk, the matrix p(A) is defined by substitution:

p(A)=a0I+a1A+⋯+akAk .

Cayley–Hamilton then says every polynomial function of an n× n matrix can be reduced to degree at most n−1. For 3×3:

p(A)=aI+bA+cA2

for some scalars a,b,c determined by dividing p by the characteristic polynomial. That is exactly the reduction skill of §2.6.5.

Non-polynomial functions, via diagonalisation

If A is diagonalisable, A=M−1DM, then for any function f defined at the eigenvalues,

f(A)=M−1f(D)M=M−1diag(f(λ1),…,f(λn))M

because f(A)=f(M−1DM)=M−1f(D)M (expand the power series, or apply the polynomial case and take limits). This defines eA, sin A, √A (when eigenvalues are positive), and At for non-integer t.

4.3 Linear transformations

Enrichment — beyond the prescribed 5 hours.

Definition

A map T:ℝn→ℝm is linear if, for all vectors u,v and all scalars c,d,

T(cu+dv)=cT(u)+dT(v).

Equivalently: T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v) and T(cu)=cT(u). Two immediate consequences:

T(0)=0,   T(c1v1+⋯+ckvk)=c1T(v1)+⋯+ckT(vk).

The second says a linear map is completely determined by what it does to a basis.

Every linear map is a matrix map

Let e1,…,en be the standard basis. Define

A=[ T(e1)  T(e2)  ⋯  T(en) ].

Then for every x,

T(x)=Ax .

A is called the standard matrix of T. So "linear transformation" and "matrix" are two views of the same object — which is why eigenvalues, defined for matrices, are really properties of linear maps.

Kernel and image

ker T={x:T(x)=0},   imT={T(x):x∈ℝn}.

T is injective (one-to-one) ⟺ker T={0}. For an n× n matrix, injective ⟺ surjective ⟺ invertible ⟺ no zero eigenvalue.

Eigenvalues as invariant directions

If T(v)=λ v with v≠0, then T stretches v by λ and does not rotate it. This is the geometric meaning of an eigenvector:

  • **Reflection in the line y=x:** A=0110. The line y=x is fixed (eigenvalue 1); the perpendicular direction y=−x is flipped (eigenvalue −1).
  • **Rotation by 90∘:** A=0−110. Characteristic polynomial λ2+1=0 has no real roots, which matches the geometry: a quarter-turn moves every real direction.

Exam relevance: the definition and the standard matrix are the examinable core; the eigenvalue interpretation is the conceptual link back to §2.

Where to read more. ILA §3.3, "Linear Transformations", covers the definition, how to test linearity, and the standard matrix, with worked examples. This is the best beginner treatment among the sources used here.

4.4 Quadratic forms

Enrichment — beyond the prescribed 5 hours.

Definition and matrix

A quadratic form in x1,…,xn is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 2:

Q(x1,…,xn)=∑i,jaijxixj .

It can always be written

Q(x)=xTAx

with A symmetric. If your matrix is not symmetric, replace it by 12(A+AT): only the symmetric part contributes, because xTAx is a scalar and equals its own transpose.

Example. Q=2x12+2x22+2x1x2 comes from

A=2112,

because xTAx=2x12+2x1x2+2x22. Note the cross-term coefficient is split equally between the (1,2) and (2,1) entries.

Canonical form via eigenvalues

For a symmetric A, there is an orthogonal matrix P (so P−1=PT) with

PTAP=diag(λ1,…,λn).

Substituting x=Py gives the canonical form

Q=λ1y12+λ2y22+⋯+λnyn2 .

No cross terms remain. This is the "diagonalisation" of a quadratic form, and it is why symmetric matrices are special: their eigenvectors can be chosen mutually perpendicular.

Definiteness

NameEigenvalue conditionMeaning
Positive definiteall λi>0Q>0 for every x≠0
Negative definiteall λi<0Q<0 for every x≠0
Indefiniteboth signs occurQ takes both signs
Positive/negative semidefiniteλi≥0 / λi≤0, with a zeroQ vanishes in some nonzero direction

Worked example. For Q=2x12+2x22+2x1x2, the matrix 2112 has eigenvalues 3 and 1. Both are positive, so the form is positive definite, and in rotated coordinates

Q=3y12+y22 .

Why this is in the syllabus at all. Quadratic forms are how ML models measure "energy" or "error" (least squares, the Hessian in optimisation, covariance matrices). The eigenvalue test for definiteness is the practical payoff of Module I.

Where to read more. Nicholson §8.9, "An Application to Quadratic Forms", diagonalises quadratic forms using orthogonal matrices. Karpagam Academy notes, Ch. 5 (local PDF, pages 48–57) give the Indian-engineering treatment, including reduction by orthogonal transformation.

7.2 Mistakes specific to Cayley–Hamilton

  • Forgetting to verify. The question says "verify", so computing A2 and A3 and substituting is compulsory; quoting the theorem is not a verification.
  • **Skipping A3.** For a 3×3 matrix, the identity involves A3; you must display it.
  • **Wrong sign on the S1 term.** With S1=−1 (T10), the equation is λ3+λ2−18λ−40=0, not λ3−λ2−….
  • Reduction without division. If you "guess" the remainder, you lose the method marks. Show p(λ)=c(λ)q(λ)+r(λ).
  • **Forgetting I in the remainder.** A linear remainder 2λ−1 becomes 2A−I, not 2A−1.

7.3 Mistakes specific to diagonalisation

  • Stopping at eigenvalues. "Show it is diagonalisable" requires the multiplicity comparison (or the count of independent eigenvectors), then M and D.
  • **Building M from row vectors. Eigenvectors are columns** of M.
  • Normalising badly. Any nonzero scalar multiple is a valid eigenvector; do not "fix" a vector to unit length unless asked.
  • **Claiming M−1AM=D without checking det M≠0.** The columns must be independent; say so.
  • **Writing D=MAM−1.** The correct relation is D=M−1AM, equivalently AM=MD.
  • **Mixing up A and D.** The diagonal entries of D are eigenvalues of A; M is not unique.

8.3 The four recipes

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  1. | A−λ I|=0 → λ's.
  2. For each λ: row-reduce A−λ I.
  3. n−r parameters → eigenvectors.
  4. Check Ax=λ x.

Cayley–Hamilton verification

  1. Characteristic polynomial.
  2. A2, A3.
  3. Substitute; show zero matrix.

Inverse or high power via Cayley–Hamilton

  1. An+⋯+c0I=0.
  2. Multiply by A−1 → solve for A−1; or by A → solve for An+1.
  3. Substitute the numerical matrices.

Diagonalisation

  1. Eigenvalues and multiplicities.
  2. Eigenvector basis for each eigenvalue.
  3. Total =n? If no → not diagonalisable.
  4. M=[X1 ⋯ Xn], D=diag(λ1,…,λn) in matching order.
  5. Check AM=MD.

8.5 Ten-second sanity checks

  • Do the eigenvalues sum to the trace?
  • Does their product equal the determinant?
  • Is det M≠0?
  • Does AM=MD hold (cheaper than M−1AM=D)?
  • Does Ax=λ x hold for each eigenvector?
  • Is every eigenvector nonzero?
  • Did the constant term in Cayley–Hamilton get an I?
  • For a repeated eigenvalue, did you compute both a and g?

8.6 The five things examiners reward

  1. The characteristic polynomial written explicitly.
  2. Row reduction shown, not just the final vector.
  3. The n−r count stated.
  4. The multiplicity comparison, in words, for diagonalisability.
  5. The explicit M and D, with the order matching.
